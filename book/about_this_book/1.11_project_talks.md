# Giving a scientific talk

A result that no one understands does not change what others think or do. Talks are where much of science is shared, questioned and remembered: at group meetings, conferences, job interviews, and in front of the people who use or fund the work. A clear, compelling talk lets others build on your work, tells you through their questions what they did not understand, and is one of the skills by which colleagues and employers judge a scientist. Engaging an audience is also rewarding: explaining a question you care about to a room that follows you is one of the most energizing parts of research, and preparing the talk often shows you what you understand and what you do not.

This page gives a story structure that works for most scientific talks, what changes when the work involves machine learning, slide-design rules for geoscience figures, and how two speakers share one talk. In this course it applies to the **project proposal** (Fri Oct 30, 6 minutes plus 2 of questions), the **final presentation** (Dec 11 and finals week, 12 to 15 minutes), and the paper-pulse talks; the two templates below are for the project talks. A first talk rarely does all of this; the feedback you receive refers to these points so the next one is better.

## What makes a talk worth listening to

Know who is in the room and what they already know. In this course your audience is your classmates and the instructors: scientists who know some machine learning and different parts of the Earth. Any audience will remember one question, one figure, and one sentence. Plan those three first.

1. **One question the audience did not know they had.** Not a topic ("machine learning for landslides") but a question about the Earth: "Can a model trained on Washington landslides warn of the next one in Oregon?" Put it in your first minute.
2. **The gap before the answer.** Say what is known, what is not, and why it is hard. A talk that opens with "we found X" has nothing left to say after minute two.
3. **A human-scale anchor.** Attach each physical quantity to something the audience can picture at least once: not "0.3 g of ground acceleration" but "the floor of a building swaying enough to knock items off shelves".
4. **Evidence shown, then read.** Tell the audience what to look for before the figure appears ("watch the station in the east"), then show it. For a key result, ask them to predict it first.
5. **A last sentence, not "Questions?"** End on the one sentence you want the room to repeat, over your strongest figure.

## When the work involves machine learning

- **Show the data before the model.** A map of the stations, one raw sample, the number of independent units. The audience cannot judge a model on data they have not seen.
- **Name what one sample is and what the model predicts**, in one sentence: "One sample is a 60-second three-component record; the target is earthquake or noise."
- **The baseline before the network.** The simplest method that could answer the question, and its score. A deep model is interesting only by how much it beats the baseline.
- **Make the out-of-domain test the decisive result.** Training and validation show the model learned your domain; the test outside it (a new region, a later period, larger events) shows whether it generalizes. Say which axis you tested.
- **Every number with its uncertainty and its test set.** "Average precision 0.74 on held-out months at three gauges, 0.20 at an unseen gauge" is a result; "our model reaches 95% accuracy" is not.
- **Describe what the model does, not what it "understands".** "The classifier separates earthquakes from noise using energy above 5 Hz" rather than "the model learned what an earthquake is".
- **Show one failure.** A sample the model gets wrong and why. It builds more trust than another success.
- **One sentence of AI-use disclosure**: which assistant did what in your project, and what you checked.

## Template A: proposal talk, 6 minutes, two speakers

The shape is the short three-part arc: the system as it is, what is missing, what your project will change.

| # | Purpose | Slide title (state the point) | Visual | Speaker | Time |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | The question | Your question as a sentence: "Can [data] tell us [quantity] at [place]?" | One striking figure or photo of the system, team names small | A | 0:40 |
| 2 | Why it matters | What depends on the answer: "[Process] controls [hazard, resource, budget] for [who]" | Map of the study area with a scale bar and a regional inset | A | 0:50 |
| 3 | What is known and what is missing | "[Study 1] and [Study 2] did [X]; neither tested [Y]" | Split layout: the two nearest published studies, one line each on data, method, result; your difference highlighted | A | 1:00 |
| 4 | The data | "[N] [units] of [data type] from [provider], [years]" | One raw sample (a waveform, an image patch, a time series) and the count of independent units | B | 1:00 |
| 5 | Is it AI-ready? | "Our data score [x]/22; partitions will hold out [region, period or event size]" | The rubric scores as a compact table or bar, with the two weakest criteria marked; the out-of-domain axis drawn | B | 1:10 |
| 6 | The plan and the last sentence | "If [method] beats [baseline] on [held-out set], we will know [what]" | Pipeline in five steps (data, baseline, model, test, metric); the closing sentence at the bottom | B | 1:00 |
| | Questions | | Leave slide 6 up | A and B | 2:00 |

**Hand-off line** between slide 3 and slide 4 (the seam between the two speakers): connect the gap to the data, for example "Those two studies used one seismic network; Ana will show the data that let us test the method on three."

**Opening line options** (slide 1): start from an observation, not from a literature summary.
- "In [year], [event] happened at [place], and none of the [existing approach] saw it coming."
- "This is one day of [data] at [station]. Somewhere in it is [signal]. Today it takes [person or method] [time] to find it."
- "[Number] [stations or images] have been recording [process] for [years]. Nobody has asked them [question]."

**Closing line**: the sentence on slide 6 that states what you will know if the project works. Say it slowly, then stop.

## Template B: final presentation, 12 to 15 minutes

The shape is the research talk: puzzle, approach, evidence, meaning. Allow about one minute per slide, longer for the key result.

| Act | Share | Slides | What to show |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1. The puzzle | 10% | 1 to 2 | The question and why it matters, with a map and one observation that creates tension |
| 2. Data and approach | 20% | 3 to 5 | The data (sample, units, provider, AI-readiness scores), the partitions with the out-of-domain axis, the baseline and the model in one pipeline figure |
| 3. Results | 40% | 6 to 10 | One claim per slide: the baseline score; the model within the domain; the model out of the domain (the decisive slide, give it the most time); one failure case; the compute cost of training and inference |
| 4. What it means | 15% | 11 to 12 | The physical interpretation, the main limitation, and the most likely objection with your answer |
| 5. Landing | 15% | 13 to 14 | What a reader or user can now do, the open question someone in the room could take on, and your closing sentence over your best figure |

Every result slide follows the same order: what is plotted, what it shows, what it implies.

## Slide design

**Layouts that work for project talks**

```
Title as claim (results)          Split (comparison, before and after)
┌──────────────────────────┐      ┌────────────┬────────────┐
│ CLAIM AS A SENTENCE      │      │ A: label   │ B: label   │
│                          │      │ [figure]   │ [figure]   │
│      [ONE FIGURE]        │      │ key number │ key number │
│ source, one line         │      └────────────┴────────────┘
└──────────────────────────┘

Map and inset (study area)        Pipeline (method)
┌──────────────────┬───────┐      ┌──────────────────────────┐
│ [MAP, scale bar] │ inset │      │ CLAIM                    │
│                  │ or key│      │ ●────●────●────●────●    │
│                  │ number│      │ 1    2    3    4    5    │
└──────────────────┴───────┘      └──────────────────────────┘
                                   1 data, 2 baseline, 3 model,
                                   4 out-of-domain test, 5 metric
```

**Rules**

- **The title is the claim**, a full sentence: "Average precision drops from 0.74 to 0.20 at an unseen gauge", not "Results".
- **One claim and at most three visual elements per slide.** A fourth element means a new slide.
- **Label lines and points directly** on the figure; remove the legend when you can.
- **Text at least 18 pt, and nothing the audience must read below 14 pt** (axis labels included). If it does not fit, remove content, do not shrink it. At most about 60 words per slide.
- **Maps** carry a scale bar and a regional inset; stations as inverted triangles, earthquakes as circles or stars scaled by magnitude; anomalies on a diverging colormap centered at zero.
- **Colormaps**: perceptually uniform (viridis, cividis, magma) for magnitudes, diverging (RdBu) for signed values; never jet or rainbow.
- **Machine-learning figures**: loss curves with both training and validation; confusion matrices with the numbers printed in the cells; every score labeled with the set it was computed on.
- **Uncertainty**: error bars or shaded bands on every estimate; a number without its uncertainty is an assertion.
- **The same template for both speakers**: fonts, colors and figure style do not change at the hand-off.
- **The newspaper test**: cover the speaker. Can someone read the slide and get its point? If not, redesign it.

## Delivery

- **Rehearse aloud at least twice with a timer**, together. Talks run 20 to 30% long the first time.
- **Say what to look for, then show the figure**: the axes, what one point or line is, then what it shows.
- **Vary the pace**: move quickly through context, slow down on the question and on the decisive result, and pause after the sentence you want remembered.
- **Speak to the room, not the screen.** Point at the part of the figure you mean, then turn back.
- **Finish on time.** Cut a slide rather than speak faster; stopping on time is part of the grade.

## Two speakers

- **Split by act, not by slide.** In Template A, speaker A carries the question, why it matters and the gap; speaker B the data, the readiness and the plan. Each speaks at least two minutes.
- **The hand-off connects the two parts in one sentence** that names the next speaker and what they will show. Avoid "next slide please" and "I'll pass it to my partner".
- **Rehearse the transitions**, not only your own part: the hand-off is where shared talks lose time.
- **Decide who advances the slides** and stand so both of you can see the screen and the room. The speaker who is not talking looks at the slides or at the speaker.
- **Split the questions in advance**: who answers on the science, on the data, on the methods. The other adds one sentence at most.

## Answering questions

- Repeat or rephrase the question so everyone hears it and you answer the one that was asked.
- Answer in one or two sentences, then stop. "We do not know yet; we will test it by …" is a good answer.
- Write the questions down afterwards: they show what your audience did not understand.

## Common problems and their fixes

| Problem | Fix |
|---|---|
| Opening with "In the field of …, researchers have shown …" | Open with your question or one observation |
| A figure shown without saying what to look for | Name the feature, then advance to the figure |
| "Conclusions" slide with five bullets | Close with one sentence over your best figure |
| A model score with no baseline and no test set named | Show the baseline score next to it and say which set |
| Only results inside the training domain | Add the out-of-domain test, or say it is the next step and why |
| Slides crowded with text the speaker reads aloud | Keep the claim on the slide and the details in your notes |
| Every slide given the same time | Spend most time on the question and the decisive result |

Feedback uses the presentation criteria of the [final project](1.10_MLGEO_FinalProject.md): content, structure, clarity and delivery, visual aids, engagement, critical thinking, and time. For the proposal talks the feedback is formative: it prepares the final presentations.
