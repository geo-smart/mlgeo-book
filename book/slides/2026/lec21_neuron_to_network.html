One neuron: a weighted sum, then an activation

Score \(z = \sum_i w_i x_i + b\): each input times its weight, added up, plus a bias. The activation turns the score into the output.

Two minutes. Walk the figure left to right. Inputs: the numbers that describe one data sample, either features computed from a record or the samples of the record itself. Weights: one per input. The neuron multiplies, adds, and adds one more number, the bias, which shifts the score up or down (one bias per neuron, not one per input). The activation turns the score into the output. Define aloud: weight, bias, score, activation function.

The activation names the model

The same weighted sum gives three models you know. Only the last step differs.

Two minutes. Three choices for the activation, three models the class has met. No activation: the output is the score, and the neuron is the linear regression of lecture 13. Sigmoid: the output is a probability between 0 and 1, and the neuron is the logistic regression of lecture 16. Step: the output is 1 if the score is positive and 0 otherwise; this is the threshold logic unit (TLU) of McCulloch and Pitts (1943, Bull. Math. Biophys. 5, 115-133). Rosenblatt (1958) gave it a learning rule, and a layer of such units is what he called the perceptron. The TLU is still studied today, in binarized and spiking networks (slide 22). The word survives today in multilayer perceptron, the fully connected network of Part 2. A fourth use of the same sum comes next: template matching, where the weights are not fitted at all.

Template matching 1: cut out a known earthquake

The template is 6 s of record around an earthquake we already know: 600 numbers. Is the same waveform anywhere else in the record?

Two minutes. Start from what a seismologist has: the continuous record of one station (here 60 s, synthetic) and a catalog that lists one earthquake in it, at 10 s. Cut out the 6 s of record that hold it: the P wave, then the larger and slower S wave. Sampled 100 times per second, that is 600 numbers. This piece of record is the template. Nothing is fitted; it is copied from the data. Why it is useful: an earthquake that repeats on the same fault patch, recorded at the same station, sends nearly the same waveform, larger or smaller. So the question becomes: where else in the record does this shape occur? Ask the room to look at the record between 20 and 60 s and say where they see another earthquake. (There is one at 38 s, with a peak 2.5 times the noise; most will not see it.) The 600 numbers will be used as 600 weights: that is the link with the neuron of the previous slides.

Template matching 2: slide it, compute a score

One score at each position of the template: together they are the cross-correlation. The score is 0.61 at B, where an earthquake hides in the noise.

Three minutes. Place the template on the record at some position, compare it with the 6 s of record under it, and write down one number, the score (how it is computed is the next slide). Move the template by one sample and repeat. The curve of the score against the position is the cross-correlation of the template with the record; students computed one in section 2.9. Top: the template drawn at three of the positions, A, B and C. Bottom: the score at all 5,400 positions, with A, B and C marked. Read it with the room. At 10 s the template lies on the earthquake it was cut from: score 1.00, the largest possible. At A and C there is only noise under the template: 0.12 and 0.16. At B the score is 0.61, far above everything around it: the same waveform is there, too small to see by eye in the top panel. The dashed line is the detection threshold, 0.36: the largest score this template reaches on 10 minutes of noise with no earthquake. Every position above the threshold is declared a detection. Note the fast oscillation of the score around each earthquake: one sample too early or too late and the score drops, which the next slide explains.

Template matching 3: aligned waveforms score high

Score = mean of record × template, sample by sample. Aligned, peaks meet peaks and the products are positive: 0.61. Shifted or on noise, they cancel: 0.09 and 0.12.

Four minutes; this is the slide that explains the method. The score is computed in three moves: standardize the template and the 6 s of record under it (subtract the mean, divide by the standard deviation), multiply them sample by sample, and take the mean of the 600 products. That mean is the correlation coefficient of lecture 7, between −1 and 1. The figure zooms on 2.2 s around the S wave. Top row: the two waveforms. Bottom row: their product at each sample, green where it is positive, red where it is negative.

Left, the template at position B, aligned with the hidden earthquake. Where the template is up, the record is up: positive times positive is positive. Where the template is down, the record is down: negative times negative is also positive. Every peak and every trough adds to the sum, and the mean is 0.61. It is not 1 because the record also holds noise.

Middle, the template moved by 0.08 s, a quarter of the 0.33 s period of the S wave. Now the peaks of one fall on the zero crossings of the other: half of the products are positive, half negative, and they cancel. The mean is 0.09. Move by half a period and peaks meet troughs: the score is negative. This is the fast oscillation seen on the previous slide.

Right, position A, noise only. The noise goes up and down with no relation to the template: positive and negative products in equal amounts, mean 0.12.

The link to say aloud: a sum of inputs times fixed numbers is the weighted sum of the neuron. Template matching is a neuron whose 600 weights are the template, and whose bias is minus the threshold. The weights were copied from one earthquake instead of being found by training.

The 1958 rule: update the weights after each nonzero error

Error = prediction − observation: −1 for a missed event, +1 for a false alarm, 0 otherwise. From random weights the line stops moving after 48 updates.

Three minutes. Rosenblatt’s rule (1958), the first learning rule for a neuron, written out in notebook 4.0 section 4.2. Each point is a 30 s window described by two detector features: the STA/LTA ratio (short-term over long-term average amplitude, lecture 9) and the spectral centroid. The observation y is 1 for an event and 0 for noise. Start from random weights drawn with a seed (seed 0), not from zeros. Show one window and compute the prediction with the step, 0 or 1. The error is the prediction minus the observation. If it is zero, do nothing. If the neuron predicted noise and the window holds an event, the error is −1, and the rule adds the window’s features to the weights, so that the score of similar windows goes up; if it predicted an event and the window is noise, the error is +1 and the rule subtracts them. In code: error = yhat_i - y_i, then w = w - learningRate * error * x_i. The dashed lines are the boundary (where the score is zero) after 1, 5 and 15 updates; the thick line is where it stops. All 48 updates happen in the first epoch (one pass through the 600 windows); in the second epoch every error is zero. Say what the notebook did to get this result: it removed the windows in the overlap band, so that a separating line exists. The rule is guaranteed to stop only then. Ask the room: what will happen on real events, which overlap?

Why the rule cannot settle: the step gives no slope

With the step, the only measure is how many windows are wrong: flat, then a jump, so no direction to move. With the sigmoid, the log-loss has a slope everywhere and the boundary settles.

Three minutes; this is the idea Part 2 and every network rest on. Training needs two things: a number that says how wrong the neuron is, and the direction in which to move each weight to make that number smaller. The direction is the slope of the number with respect to the weight: the gradient.

Top left, the step. With an output of 0 or 1, the only measure of how wrong the neuron is: the share of windows with a nonzero error. Drawn against one weight (the weight of STA/LTA, the other weight and the bias held fixed), on 40 of the windows so the steps show: the curve is flat, then jumps when the line crosses one window, then flat again. The slope is zero on the flat parts and undefined at the jumps. Nothing tells the weight which way to go, or how far. The 1958 rule gets around this by moving the weights by a fixed amount for each window on the wrong side. When no line puts every window on the right side, some window is always wrong and the line keeps moving.

Bottom left, the sigmoid. The output is a probability, the error ŷ − y is a number between −1 and 1, and the mean log-loss is a smooth curve with a slope at every point (dashed tangent). The slope gives the direction and the size of the update, and it shrinks to zero at the lowest point, so the updates shrink and stop.

Right, both trainings on the 1,500 overlapping training windows, from the same random starting weights (seed 0). The direction of the boundary line at the end of each epoch. The rule (red) jumps between 8° and 30° for 300 epochs. Gradient descent on the log-loss (green) moves smoothly and settles near 16°, still drifting slowly.

Why this matters for networks. A hidden neuron’s weights are trained by the chain rule from the output back to the input (backpropagation, Rumelhart, Hinton and Williams 1986). Every link in the chain multiplies by the slope of an activation. With a step, that slope is zero and nothing reaches the hidden weights. This is why networks use smooth activations: sigmoid, tanh, ReLU. A second limit is separate and is not fixed by the sigmoid: one neuron, whatever its activation, separates its two answers with a straight line. Hidden layers fix that (Wednesday, Part 3). Numbers: figs/lec21_neuron/numbers_step_vs_sigmoid.json, from figscripts/lec21_step_vs_sigmoid.py.

From the rule to the loss: the error becomes smooth

Replace the step by the sigmoid and the 1958 update becomes the gradient update on the log-loss of lecture 16. Every network in Chapter 4 is trained with this update.

Three minutes. The two updates, to write on the board, with y the observation and ŷ the prediction: 1958 rule, w = w - rate * (yhat - y) * x with yhat equal to 0 or 1; log-loss, the same line with yhat the probability from the sigmoid. The figure shows the size of the error for a window that contains an event (y = 1), against the score the neuron gave it. Red, the rule: an error of 1 if the score is negative (a missed event), 0 if it is positive. Green, the log-loss: an event predicted with confidence barely moves the weights, a confident miss moves them almost as much as under the rule, and everything in between is proportional. This is exact: differentiate the log-loss with respect to a weight, for a sigmoid neuron, and the result is (ŷ − y) times the input, the error times the input. Notebook 3.6 checked that derivative against automatic differentiation. A smooth activation and a loss to lower are what made networks with several layers trainable. Wednesday takes this update apart one step at a time: loss, gradient, learning rate.

Part 2. How are the weights found? Start from random weights

Travel time against distance: the neuron predicts w × distance + b. Its weight w and bias b start as random numbers drawn with a seed, not as zeros.

Wednesday starts here. Three minutes. Say it plainly: Monday went too fast over how the weights are found. Today we take gradient descent one step at a time, with the words and symbols of notebook 3.6. Write them on the board and leave them up: observation \(y_i\), prediction \(\hat{y}_i\), error \(e_i = \hat{y}_i - y_i\), loss \(\mathcal{L}\), gradient \(\partial \mathcal{L} / \partial w\), learning rate \(\alpha\).

The geoscience. One earthquake, P-wave arrivals picked at 80 stations between 5 and 150 km. In a crust of uniform velocity the travel time is a line in distance, t = t0 + d / v, and its slope is the slowness 1 / v. Synthetic, written in notebook 4.0 section 2: 6 km/s, a delay of 1 s, 0.5 s of noise on each pick. Each station is one sample. The model is Monday’s neuron with no activation: one input x (the distance, standardized), one weight w, one bias b.

Starting weights. Gradient descent improves weights, so they need a starting value. We draw w and b from a normal distribution with mean 0 and standard deviation 1. The three lines are what three seeds give before any training. Two questions for the room. Why not zeros? In a network, hidden neurons that start with the same weights receive the same gradient at every update and stay copies of each other; random weights make them differ. One neuron alone would train from zero, but we keep the habit every network needs. Why a seed? The same seed returns the same random numbers, so the run can be repeated exactly; the seed is reported with the result.

Errors: prediction minus observation

Each sample has an error: its prediction minus its observation. With the starting weights every prediction is too short: −24.2 s at the farthest station.

Two minutes. Seed 0: w = 0.13, b = −0.13, the blue line. For each of the 80 samples, the prediction is the point on the line and the observation is the black dot. The error is the prediction minus the observation, in seconds: the red bar. It has a sign. Here all errors are negative, because the line lies below every observation, and they grow with distance. Ask the room to read two errors off the figure: a near station (about −2 s) and the farthest one (−24.2 s). Notebook 4.0 section 2.2: students write error = yhat - y. The word is error, as in the statistics of lectures 12 and 13: the difference between a prediction and an observation.

The loss of each sample, and its mean over the batch

Loss of a sample: its error squared. In this batch of 16 samples it ranges from 14 to 608 s². The loss of the batch is their mean, 248 s².

Three minutes. The loss turns the errors into one number to make small. First, one loss per sample: the squared error, \(\mathcal{L}_i = (\hat{y}_i - y_i)^2\), in s². The bars are 16 of the 80 samples, in order of distance. The loss differs a great deal from sample to sample: 14 s² for the station at 9 km, 608 s² for a station at 143 km. Then one number for the batch: the mean of the losses of its samples, 248 s² here, the dashed line. A batch is the set of samples used for one update of the weights; it can be all the training samples (the mean over the 80 is 269 s²) or a subset, which comes four slides later. Two things to say. The mean is what training lowers, so samples with large errors count the most, and squaring makes that stronger. And the same construction holds for a classification: one log-loss per sample (lecture 16), then the mean. Notebook 4.0 section 2.3.

The chain rule: multiply the links

When A changes B and B changes C, the change of C with A is the product of the two links. The gradient of the loss is such a product: input × 2 × error = −74.5 for the farthest station.

Three minutes; every gradient in the chapter is computed this way, so take it slowly. Start with the geoscience row. Along a borehole, temperature rises with depth, 25 °C per km, and P-wave velocity falls with temperature, about 0.0005 km/s per °C (round values for the example). How fast does velocity change with depth? Go one link at a time: one km deeper is 25 °C warmer, and 25 °C warmer is 25 × 0.0005 = 0.0125 km/s slower. The change along the chain is the product of the changes along each link. That is the chain rule: dv/dz = dv/dT × dT/dz.

Row 2, the neuron of the travel-time example, at its random starting weights (seed 0), for the farthest station (147 km). The weight changes the prediction: the prediction is w × x + b, so raising w by a little raises the prediction by x times as much; x = 1.54 for this station. The prediction changes the loss of the sample: the loss is error², so its change with the prediction is 2 × error = −48.5 (the prediction is 24.2 s too short). Multiply: −74.5. Raising w by a small amount and measuring the loss gives the same number. This answers the question “why is the input in the gradient”: the input is the link from the weight to the prediction; it is the gradient of the prediction, not of the loss.

Row 3: with an activation, there is one more link, the slope of the activation, between the score and the prediction. The next two slides use this row. In a network, each hidden layer adds links, and the backward pass of Part 3 is this product, computed from the loss back to every weight.

The gradient of the loss: one number for each weight

Gradient of the loss for weight \(w_j\): the mean over the samples of 2 × error × input. Here −13.6: raising \(w\) lowers the loss.

Four minutes; this is the slide to spend time on. Question: if I raise w a little, does the loss go up or down, and by how much? That number is the gradient of the loss with respect to w, \(\partial \mathcal{L} / \partial w\). As in notebook 3.6: positive if the loss increases when w increases, negative if it decreases.

Right panel first. The loss drawn against w, with the bias held fixed: a curve with one lowest point. We are at the red dot (w = 0.13). The gradient is the slope of the curve there, −13.6: the loss goes down by about 13.6 s² for each unit added to w. So w should go up.

Left panel: where the number comes from. For one sample, the loss is \((w x_i + b - y_i)^2\), and the chain rule gives \(2 e_i x_i\): twice the error times the input. Each sample has its own gradient (the bars; same 16 samples as before). Stations nearer than the mean distance have a negative input x, so their gradient is positive; the far stations, with large errors, give large negative gradients. The loss is the mean of the losses of the samples, so its gradient is the mean of their gradients: the dashed line. There is one such number for each weight: here two, −13.6 for w and −29.9 for b. A network with 2,116 weights has 2,116 of them, computed the same way by the chain rule (backpropagation). Notebook 4.0 section 2.4 checks the −13.6 by raising w by 0.0001 and measuring the change of the loss.

Gradient of the loss = error part × slope × input

Gradient of one sample’s loss, for three activations. Their mean over the batch, times − learning rate, is the change of the weight (next slide).

Three minutes. The general formula, for one sample i and weight j. The neuron computes the score \(z_i = \sum_j w_j x_{ij} + b\), then the prediction \(\hat{y}_i = \phi(z_i)\). The loss of the sample depends on the weight through the prediction, and the prediction through the score. The chain rule multiplies the three links: \(\partial \mathcal{L}_i / \partial w_j = \partial \mathcal{L}_i / \partial \hat{y}_i \times \phi'(z_i) \times x_{ij}\). In words: how the loss changes with the prediction (the error part), times how the prediction changes with the score (the slope of the activation), times how the score changes with the weight (the input). The gradient of the loss is the mean over the samples of the batch, as on the previous slide.

Read the three columns. Top row: the activation. Middle row: its slope.

No activation (left), with the squared error. The error part is \(2 e_i\) and the slope is 1 everywhere: the gradient is \(2 e_i x_{ij}\), error times input. This is the previous slide and notebook 4.0 section 2.4.

Step (middle). The slope is zero everywhere except at z = 0, where it is undefined. Whatever the loss, the gradient is zero: no update, the weights never move. Rosenblatt’s rule of 1958 works around it by setting the slope to 1 by hand, which gives the update \((\hat{y}_i - y_i) x_{ij}\) with a prediction of 0 or 1. It has the form of a gradient step but it is not the gradient of any loss with a lowest point, which is why it never settles when the classes overlap (slide 12).

Sigmoid (right), with the log-loss of lecture 16. The error part of the log-loss is \((\hat{y}_i - y_i) / (\hat{y}_i (1 - \hat{y}_i))\) and the slope of the sigmoid is \(\hat{y}_i (1 - \hat{y}_i)\): they cancel, and the gradient is again error times input, \((\hat{y}_i - y_i) x_{ij}\). That cancellation is why the log-loss goes with the sigmoid. With the squared error instead, the slope would stay in the gradient, and a confidently wrong prediction (where the sigmoid is flat) would get almost no correction.

Say it explicitly: the bottom row is the gradient for one sample, not the change of the weight. The change comes in two more steps: take the mean of these gradients over the samples of the batch, then multiply by minus the learning rate: Δw_j = −α × mean_i(∂L_i/∂w_j). For no activation that is −α × mean(2 e_i x_ij), for the sigmoid with the log-loss −α × mean((ŷ_i − y_i) x_ij), and for the step 0. The next slide shows these two steps with numbers.

The link to Part 3: in a network the chain has one more link per layer, and every link contains the slope of an activation. That is backpropagation, slides 33 to 35.

The threshold logic unit today

Binarized and spiking networks keep the threshold logic unit for the prediction. To train them, the backward pass replaces its zero slope by a stand-in: Rosenblatt’s trick, made systematic.

Two minutes; the answer to “is the step function dead?” No. Two research lines keep it, for hardware reasons. Binarized networks restrict activations, and often weights, to +1 or −1 with the sign function: a network then needs one bit per number and replaces multiplications by cheap operations, which matters on low-power chips, for example on sensors deployed in the field. Spiking networks copy the biological neuron: it emits a spike, a 1, when its membrane potential crosses a threshold, and stays silent otherwise; neuromorphic chips run them with very little energy. Both hit the problem of slide 21: the slope of the step is zero, so no gradient. Both solve it the same way: keep the step in the forward pass, and in the backward pass use a stand-in slope, called a surrogate. Blue: the straight-through estimator, slope 1 where the score is within 1 of the threshold and 0 beyond, used by Courbariaux, Hubara, Soudry, El-Yaniv and Bengio (2016, NeurIPS, arXiv:1602.02830) for binarized networks, after Bengio, Léonard and Courville (2013, arXiv:1308.3432). Green: a smooth surrogate peaked at the threshold, the fast sigmoid of SuperSpike (Zenke and Ganguli 2018, Neural Computation 30, 1514-1541); the review that made the method widely used is Neftci, Mostafa and Zenke (2019, IEEE Signal Processing Magazine 36(6), 51-63). Rosenblatt’s 1958 rule did the same thing by hand: it set the slope to 1. Geoscience applications of spiking networks exist but are early (for example microseismic detection on distributed acoustic sensing data, a 2024 conference chapter); I have not seen them benchmarked against standard detectors, so I do not show them.

The update: each weight moves against its own gradient

Change of a weight = − learning rate × its gradient. Both gradients are negative, so both values go up: w by 1.36, b by 2.99. The loss falls from 269 to 172 s².

Three minutes; this is the step where the gradient is used, and the one students most often skip. The rule, from notebook 3.6: new weight = old weight − learning rate × gradient of the loss for that weight. Read the left panel. The black curve is the loss against the weight w, with the bias held at its current value. The red dot is where we are: w = 0.13. The dashed line is the slope of the loss there, the gradient, −13.58. The minus sign in the rule turns a negative gradient into a positive change: −0.1 × (−13.58) = +1.36, so w moves right, to 1.48 (green dot), down the curve. If the gradient had been positive, the same rule would have moved w left. Either way the loss goes down: the sign of the gradient says which way is uphill, and the update goes the other way. The size of the change is the learning rate times the gradient: a steep loss gives a large change, a flat loss a small one. Right panel: the bias, with its own gradient (−29.85) and its own change (+2.99). Both move at the same time. After this one update the loss is 172 s², down from 269. Notebook 4.0 section 2.5 has the table of these numbers and the same figure, and Your turn (4) asks students to write wNew = w - learningRate * gradW.

Many updates: the path to the lowest loss

Each red dot is the weight and bias after one more update. The updates are long where the gradient is large and shrink near the lowest loss, where the gradient is zero.

Two minutes. The previous slide, repeated 60 times.

The figure is a map of the loss for every pair (w, b), dark where it is low. The red dots are the weights after each update, starting from the random weights of seed 0. Read two things. The updates are long at the start, where the gradient is large, and shorten as the gradient shrinks: the size of an update is set by the gradient, we do not choose it step by step. And the path ends at the lowest point, where the gradient is zero for both weights, so the updates stop by themselves: w = 6.92, b = 14.79. For a line, least squares gives the same two numbers in one formula. For a network there is no formula, and this procedure is all we have.

Batches: several updates in one epoch

Batches of 16 samples: 5 updates per epoch. At the last epoch the five batch losses still range from 0.12 to 0.31 s². The loss of the epoch is their mean.

Three minutes. With a large dataset, the loss and its gradient are computed on a batch, a small set of samples, and the weights are updated after each batch. Procedure at each epoch: shuffle the 80 samples, cut them into 5 batches of 16; for each batch compute the predictions, the errors, the mean loss over the 16 samples, the gradient of that mean, and update. Each blue dot is the loss of one batch; the red line is the mean of the five dots of each epoch.

Two things to read. In the first epoch the loss already falls from batch to batch (246, 189, 107, 74, 51 s²), because the weights are updated five times. At the end the weights hardly move, and the batch losses still differ, from 0.12 to 0.31 s² at the last epoch: each batch holds different samples with different noise. The loss of a batch is a noisy estimate of the loss of the training set, and so is its gradient. This is why a loss printed batch by batch jumps, and why notebooks 4.1 and 4.2 report a mean per epoch. Vocabulary: batch size (16 here, 128 in notebook 4.1), epoch. In PyTorch: a DataLoader shuffles and cuts the batches, loss.backward() computes the gradients, optimizer.step() updates the weights. Notebook 4.0 section 2.7.

Part 3. A network: neurons that feed neurons

Each hidden neuron is the same neuron, with its own weights. The output neurons take the hidden outputs as their inputs.

Two minutes. Recall in one sentence: one neuron is a weighted sum and an activation; its weights are found by gradient descent on a loss. Its limit: the boundary between its two answers is a line or a plane, and its prediction of a number is a straight line. A hidden layer is a set of neurons that all read the same inputs, each with its own weights, so each responds to its own pattern. Their outputs are the inputs of the next layer. The activation between layers matters: a weighted sum of weighted sums is one weighted sum, so without it the network reduces to one neuron. Count weights with the room on the drawing: 4 inputs to 5 hidden neurons is 4 × 5 + 5 biases = 25; 5 hidden to 2 outputs is 5 × 2 + 2 = 12. This is also where the random starting weights matter: five hidden neurons that started with equal weights would stay equal. Hornik, Stinchcombe and White (1989) proved that one hidden layer with enough neurons can approximate any continuous function. The theorem does not say how many neurons, how much data, or whether training finds the weights. This fully connected network is called a multilayer perceptron (MLP). The next slides train one with the loop of Part 2.

A simple example: temperature against depth in the ocean

🖥️ Model & simulation 🌊 Ocean 🌊 Oceanography

One neuron predicts a straight line: 2.4 °C per 100 m, error 1.0 °C. It cannot bend at the two thermoclines. Synthetic profile, 60 samples.

Two minutes. Geoscience first. The upper ocean is layered: a mixed layer stirred by wind, at nearly one temperature; a seasonal thermocline where temperature drops fast; a slower decrease through the main thermocline; cold deep water. The profile here is synthetic, written in the figure script (figscripts/lec21_network_training.py): 4 °C deep water, plus 6 °C lost across a seasonal thermocline near 60 m, plus 8 °C lost across a main thermocline near 300 m, sampled at 60 random depths between 0 and 600 m with 0.3 °C of noise, like a float profile. A second set of 40 samples with new noise is the validation set. The task: predict temperature from depth. One input, one output. The red line is the best one neuron can do with no activation: a straight line, 2.4 °C per 100 m, with a root-mean-square error of 1.0 °C, three times the noise. Ask: what shape would the model need? Steps. That is what a hidden neuron with a smooth activation provides.

Forward pass: three hidden neurons, added up

Each hidden neuron makes one smooth step in depth. Scaled by its output weight and summed, the three steps build the profile: 10 weights in all.

Three minutes. This is the trained network, read from input to output; computing the output from the input is called the forward pass. The network: 1 input (depth), 3 hidden neurons with a tanh activation, 1 output (temperature). Count the weights: 3 input weights and 3 biases for the hidden layer, 3 output weights and 1 bias: 10. Panel 1: the output of each hidden neuron against depth. Each is a smooth step between −1 and 1; its input weight sets how sharp the step is and its bias sets the depth where it occurs. Panel 2: each output multiplied by its output weight, in °C. Panel 3: their sum plus the output bias, over the 60 samples. Ask the room which hidden neuron carries the main thermocline (neuron 3, pink, centered near 280 m) and which carries the seasonal thermocline (neuron 2, green, near 70 m). Tanh is a sigmoid rescaled to run from −1 to 1; networks today more often use ReLU, max(0, z), which builds the curve from straight pieces. This is Monday’s reading again: each hidden neuron responds to one pattern, here one step in depth.

The loss of the network: the same mean of squared errors

Each red bar is the error of one sample, prediction minus observation. The loss is the mean of the squared errors; its square root here is 0.68 °C.

Two minutes. Partway through training, after 1,000 updates: the network has found one bend and misses the plateau near 150 m. For each of the 60 samples, the red bar is the error, predicted minus measured temperature. Square them, take the mean: the loss, exactly as in Part 2. Its square root is in °C, 0.68 here, which is easier to judge than the loss itself: the noise in the data is 0.3 °C, so there is still structure left to fit. For classification the loss of a sample is the log-loss of lecture 16; the rest is identical. The gradient now has 10 numbers, one for each weight, computed by the chain rule from the output back to the input, layer by layer (backpropagation; notebook 3.6 did it for one neuron).

Backpropagation for one neuron

Backward, from the error: × 2 × slope gives the gradient for b (−48.5), then × input the gradient for w (−74.5).

Three minutes. Before the network, the same picture for one neuron: the travel-time neuron of Part 2, at its random starting weights (seed 0), for the farthest station (147 km), the numbers of the chain-rule slide. Forward pass, top row: the standardized distance 1.54, times the weight 0.13, plus the bias −0.13, gives the score 0.06; there is no activation, so the prediction is 0.06 s. The observation is 24.30 s, so the error is −24.24 s. Backward pass, bottom row, read right to left: start from the error. The loss is error², so the first link multiplies by 2: −48.5. The activation is the next link back; with no activation its slope is 1, so −48.5 again. This number is the gradient for the bias, since the bias adds directly to the score. One more link back, through the weight, multiplies by the input 1.54: −74.5, the gradient for the weight. That is all backpropagation is: the chain rule of slide 19, computed from the error back to each weight, reusing the number at each node. With a sigmoid, the middle link would multiply by its slope ŷ(1 − ŷ) instead of 1 (slide 21). The next two slides do exactly this for a network with a hidden layer: more nodes, and each hidden neuron receives the error through its own connection.

Backpropagation 1: the forward pass gives the error

One sample at 142 m: depth in, each hidden neuron computes tanh of its weighted input, the output sums them. Prediction minus observation: error −0.51, or −2.0 °C.

Three minutes. The question of Part 3: the output weights touch the prediction directly, so their gradient is error times input, as for one neuron. But how do we get the gradient for the input weights of the hidden layer, which never see the error? Answer: backpropagation, the error sent back through the network. First the forward pass, for one sample, with the real numbers at the random starting weights (seed 2). Input: depth 142 m, standardized to −0.53. Each hidden neuron multiplies it by its input weight, adds its bias, applies tanh: −0.87, 0.83, 0.66. The output multiplies each by its output weight (−0.16, 0.39, 0.14), sums, adds its bias (−0.28): prediction 0.28. The observation is 0.79 (all in standardized units; temperature divided by its standard deviation, about 4 °C). Error = prediction − observation = −0.51, about −2.0 °C: the network predicts too cold at this depth. Numbers: figs/lec21_neuron/numbers_backprop.json, from figscripts/lec21_backprop.py; notebook 4.0 section 6.1 prints the same.

Backpropagation 2: the error goes back

Each hidden neuron receives the output error × the weight that links it to the output × the slope of its tanh. The gradient of any weight is the error at its end × the value at its start.

Four minutes; the slide that explains how every network is trained. Start at the output: the loss of this sample is error², and its slope with respect to the prediction is 2 × error = −1.02. Output weights: as for one neuron, gradient = −1.02 × the hidden output feeding that weight. For neuron 1: −1.02 × (−0.87) = +0.88.

Now send the error back. Hidden neuron j receives −1.02 × W2_j × (1 − h_j²). Read the three factors aloud. The output error: if the prediction were right, nothing would come back. The weight of the connection: neuron 1 is linked to the output by −0.16, a weak link, so it gets little of the blame. The slope of tanh at that neuron, 1 − h²: neuron 1 sits at −0.87, on the flat part of tanh (slope 0.25), so changing its score would barely change its output. Result: +0.04 for neuron 1, −0.13 for neuron 2, −0.08 for neuron 3. Each hidden neuron is now a single neuron with its own error, and its input weight’s gradient is its error × its input: −0.13 × (−0.53) = +0.07 for neuron 2. The update then uses these gradients exactly as in Part 2, averaged over the samples of the batch.

Three points to make. First, these are the chain-rule derivatives; the script checks every one against a small change of the weight (agreement to 1e-6), and notebook 4.0 section 6.2 does the same check. Second, the whole backward pass costs about as much as the forward pass, whatever the number of weights: this is what makes networks with millions of weights trainable. Third, the slope of the activation is a factor in every hidden error. With a step function the slope is zero and nothing comes back: that is the 1958 problem again, and the reason networks use tanh, sigmoid or ReLU. In PyTorch, loss.backward() does exactly this computation for every layer. Students write it themselves in notebook 4.0 section 6, Your turn (8): backError = 2 * error[:, None] * W2 * (1 - H ** 2).
