Partition the data, then cross-validate

Cross-validation repeats one step k times: train on k − 1 folds, score the held-out fold, move on, until every sample has been validated once. The mean of the k scores compares models. The test window stays aside and is scored once. For a forecast, every split follows time, with a gap so no target window overlaps the next.

Three minutes. Definitions first, since everything in Part 1 uses them. Partitioning means deciding which samples the model may learn from (training), which ones we use to compare models and settings (validation), and which ones we keep to report the final score (test). One train/validation split gives one noisy score; cross-validation repeats the split k times (here 5) and averages, so every training sample is validated once. Left: shuffled folds. The orange validation samples sit between blue training samples, so each validation day has its neighbors, yesterday and tomorrow, in training. Right: forecasting is its own case. Each step trains on the past, drops a gap, and validates on the next block, which is how a forecast is used; the test window is the most recent one, also behind a gap, and no information from after the forecast time enters training. The gap matters at P395: each target is a 30-day mean and samples start every 5 days, so without a gap of 6 samples the last training targets overlap the first validation days. In both panels the black test window is never touched while we choose. In code: KFold(shuffle=True) on the left, TimeSeriesSplit(n_splits=5, gap=6) on the right. Schematic, no data; drawn by figscripts/lec18_cv_diagram.py.

Validation chooses, the test reports

Partition Drawn from Used for How often we look
Training the domain fitting the model’s parameters every fit
Validation the same domain, independent units choosing models, features, settings such as tree depth; spotting overfitting many times
Test, within domain the same domain, never used to choose reporting how well the model interpolates once
Test, out of domain outside the training domain along a stated axis reporting how well it generalizes along that axis once

Same words elsewhere: a satellite product “validated” against in situ stations, and a hydrological model “validated” on a later period than its calibration, are tests here; so is forecast “verification”. Define the three terms in your report.

Report the within-domain and out-of-domain test scores separately. The best validation score is optimistic: in 3.10, .845 against .820 on the untouched test.

Two minutes. Validation and test answer different questions. Validation is for choosing, and because we choose the best of many, its score is biased upward: the more models and settings we compare, the larger the bias. The test set is for reporting, and it only stays honest if nothing was chosen with it: no settings, no features, no “let me try one more model”. If you look at the test set and then change something, it has become a validation set, and you need a new test set. Within domain and out of domain are the two kinds of test, and lecture 10 (criterion B7) asked each team to name its out-of-domain axis. On vocabulary: Two minutes. Students will read papers where “validation” means what we call a test: a satellite product “validated” against in situ stations has not been tuned on those stations, so that comparison is a test, and usually out of domain (other sites, other conditions). Hydrologists calibrate a model on one period and validate on another; Klemeš (1986, Hydrological Sciences Journal 31, 13-24) set out the split-sample test and harder versions across catchments and climates, which are out-of-domain tests in our vocabulary. Weather services call forecast scoring verification. Ask: in your discipline, what word do papers use for the held-out score? The words differ, the question is the same: was this data used to choose anything? The .845 and .820 come from the lecture 18 figure script (search_optimism).

Training scores say nothing about skill

The forest scores 1.000 on its training events and .884 on test events. One tree grown deeper fits training better and better while validation stops improving after depth 8: that widening gap is overfitting. Validation picks the depth; the test scores it once.

Three minutes; a recall of lecture 17’s “Keep asking and the tree memorizes”, now on all 61 features of the four seismic source types (canonical split of 3.5: 3,000 training and 1,000 test events). Left: a 300-tree forest fits every training event by construction, because its trees grow until their leaves are pure; only the test score, .884, says anything about skill. Right: one decision tree, its maximum depth varied from 1 to unlimited, scored by 5-fold cross-validation inside the training events. Training macro-F1 climbs to 1.000; validation macro-F1 peaks at .812 at depth 8, then drifts down to .792 at full depth (18 levels). Below depth 3 both are low: underfitting. Beyond depth 8, the tree learns the noise of its training events: overfitting, diagnosed by the gap between the two curves, never by the training score alone. Depth 8 is chosen with validation data only; the test set, untouched until then, gives .825, against .800 for the full-depth tree. This is the same loop as 3.10’s search, with one setting. Row order is not random

The seismic files are stored class by class. Taking the last 25% of rows as the test set puts all 1,000 surface events in the test and none in training: accuracy 0. Five random folds, stratified by class, give .876 to .892.

Two minutes. Files are sorted: by class, by date, by station, by latitude, by magnitude. A split by row index then gives training and test sets with different distributions of Y (and often of X), an accidental out-of-domain test. Here it is extreme: the forest never sees a surface event and gets every test event wrong. With continuous targets the same happens more quietly, for example a catalog sorted by magnitude whose last rows are the largest events. Shuffling with stratification by class fixes the mix (third and fourth bars). Cross-validation then averages over several partitions: the five validation folds range from .876 to .892, so any single split could have been reported as either. Caution for the next slide: shuffling is right here because these samples are independent events; for data correlated in time or space, shuffling leaks, and the split must follow the structure instead. Numbers from figscripts/lec18_train_val_test.py.

Three axes for an out-of-domain test

Statistical: inputs far from the training samples (lecture 17’s days above 28 °C). Spatiotemporal: a later period or a new region (P395’s future, the soil map’s 6° blocks). Physical: a process or event size not in training (floods larger than any trained on).

Three minutes; students saw this figure in lecture 10 (criterion B7), today we connect it to the scores. A model interpolates well inside its training domain; whether it generalizes is only known along the axis we held out, so a project must name that axis. Statistical: measured in the space of input features, for example the distance of each new sample to its nearest training sample, standardized with training statistics only (Meyer and Pebesma 2021 call the inside the area of applicability). Lecture 17: a forest trained below 28 °C saw its error rise from 4.1 to 7.9 °C on hotter days, while linear regression stayed at 4.0. Spatiotemporal: P395 forecasts later in time; the soil map’s held-out blocks. Physical: larger floods than any in training, a new source type, a different tectonic setting; Frame et al. (2022, corrigendum 2023) is the hydrology example from lecture 10. Ask each pair: which axis matters for the people who will use your model?

Data leakage: the test leaks into training

Leakage is any route by which information about the validation or test samples reaches the model while it is trained or chosen. Each pair is the same model and data; only the leak differs.

Three minutes. Four routes, each with a number from this course. (1) Neighbors in time: shuffled folds put yesterday and tomorrow in training, so P395’s validation error is 0.46 mm against 12.4 mm when validating on the future (lower is better). (2) Same event on both sides: one earthquake is recorded by many stations, and its magnitude is the same on every row; random rows let the forest recognize the event (R² .87 against .65 with whole events held out; synthetic table in 3.8). (3) Preprocessing on all data before the split: oversampling, scaling, feature selection or imputation fit on all samples carry test information into training; lecture 16’s oversampling gave F1 .995 against .590. (4) Choosing with the test set: the best of many tries looks better than an untouched test (.845 against .820). A fifth route, target leakage, is a feature that already contains the answer: in 3.2, 98.5% of explosions had a depth error of exactly 31.61 km, an analyst convention, so depth error “detects” explosions. The fix in every case: split first, by what the samples share, and fit every preprocessing step inside the training folds (a scikit-learn Pipeline does this). Figure from figscripts/lec18_cv_diagram.py; values from the lecture 18 and lecture 16 figure scripts.

Stock also found that block size mattered most, and block shape and the number of folds little. Point at the top right panel, RFXY: the dashed line sits near .14 and the solid line near .22, read from the figure. The colored lines are block splits, rising toward the truth as blocks grow, without reaching it. Stock also found that leaving out whole subbasins performed best overall because it matched the mapping task, although large blocks sometimes overestimated errors. The advice: use correlograms of the predictors and domain knowledge, and try a range of sizes when they do not settle it. Connects to Part 3: a search would have chosen RFXY.

Ask the class, two or three minutes, one question at a time. Points to bring out. Soil moisture: correlated over the size of the weather systems that wet it (winter frontal storms over several hundred to a thousand kilometers, summer convection over tens of kilometers), with soil texture, vegetation and topography adding variation at kilometers. On Friday our CPC field had correlation .40 at 200 to 300 km and near zero by 900 km, so 6 degree blocks (about 450 to 670 km here) are close to the scale where neighbors stop sharing information; CPC is a 0.5 degree model, so it has no kilometer-scale variation at all. P395: daily noise, the seasonal cycle of hydrological loading (one year), episodic slow slip (months to years apart along Cascadia) and steady plate motion (decades). The 30-day gap only removes overlapping windows; the seasonal cycle and the trend still connect training and validation, which is why we validate on the future rather than adding a gap to a shuffled split. Projects: the block or the gap should exceed the scale over which the target and the features stay correlated, measured with a correlogram or a variogram, and match how the model will be used (a new region, a new year, a new event).

Checklist before you train

  1. What are X and Y, and what is one sample?
  2. How are Y and X distributed: rare classes, sorted files, uneven ranges?
  3. Are samples correlated in time, in space, or by event?
  4. Which split matches how the model will be used: shuffled, forward in time with a gap, blocked in space, grouped by event?
  5. Which test is within domain, which is out of domain, and along which axis?

Report training only as a diagnosis, validation for your choices, and the two test scores separately.

Right: Shukla and colleagues decompose annual log seismic energy into modes, feed them to five learners, and let a forest combine the forecasts. A good question for any such paper, and for the room: their text describes decomposing the series, and does not say the decomposition was fit on training years only. If it used later years, what would that do to the test score? Say that this is a question to ask, not a finding: I read the methods, not their code. Voting is the simplest combination: equal votes among different models. Pasting is bagging without replacement.

Part 3, AutoML: the method is a loop

def mini_automl(candidates, X, y, cv):
    scores = {}
    for name, model in candidates.items():
        scores[name] = -cross_val_score(
            model, X, y, cv=cv,
            scoring="neg_mean_absolute_error").mean()
    return pd.Series(scores).sort_values()

The argument that decides the result is cv.

Three flaws. Find them before you open 3.10.

Give the room a minute, in pairs. One: a class is dropped under a comment about noisy labels, so the score describes an easier problem. Two: the scaler sees the test events before the split. The effect here is small, but with imputation or target encoding it can be large. Three: models are chosen on training accuracy, which rewards memorization. Warning signs: a near-perfect score, no baseline, no held-out score. This is a script written for this class, not a recorded agent output.
