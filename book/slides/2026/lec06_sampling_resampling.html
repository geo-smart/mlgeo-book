Today’s question

Downsample a series, fill a gap, average a network — each changes the data.

Can you change the sampling without inventing signal that was never measured?

Reading: 2.5 Arrays · 2.6 Resampling

Frame the session: Friday was tables; today the other two streams — gridded arrays and sensor series — and the operations that change their sampling. “Resampling” means two things and we do both: statistical resampling (bootstrap — redraw from a sample to measure uncertainty) and signal resampling (change a series’ sampling rate or grid). They share one trap: done naively, each manufactures data that was never measured. Every repair today is graded against planted truth. Ask the room: who has ever called a resample-to-daily function, or interpolated across a gap, without checking what it did? Everyone — that is today.

Three minutes. A cylinder of Clashach sandstone, 10 mm across, loaded until it fails along a shear band, imaged by synchrotron X-ray micro-CT at Diamond Light Source while it deforms. Panel a: a horizontal slice and a vertical re-slice; the zoom states the grid spacing on the figure (“1 pixel = 7.91 µm”) and the grayscale is X-ray attenuation in 16-bit counts, not a physical unit. Panel b: the same 2.3 mm window in successive tomograms (labelled ‘slice 16’ to ‘slice 34’ in the figure, but each is a full 3D volume at a later time), before and after peak stress (19); panel c: zooms of 0.8 to 1.1 mm where cracks open, grains rotate and break. In time: each volume takes about 80 s to acquire and they come every 85 s, so anything faster (the acoustic emissions, the ‘seismic events’ of the title) is invisible to the grid; the paper’s point is that the seismic events and the grain-scale mechanisms see different parts of the failure. The 15 GB is our arithmetic: 1596 × 1506 × 3080 voxels × 2 bytes.

Satellite: soil moisture before and after gap filling

🛰️ Satellite & airborne 💧 Land water 💧 Hydrology & land-surface modeling

Preimesberger et al. (2025), Earth System Science Data 17, 4305–4329, Fig. 6, doi:10.5194/essd-17-4305-2025. CC BY 4.0.

On 1 March 2007, frozen ground (hatched) and dense vegetation leave much of the land without a retrieval; filled values match in situ stations about as well as retrievals do (median R = 0.72), but they are estimates.

Three minutes. ESA Climate Change Initiative soil moisture, the upper few centimeters of soil, in m³ of water per m³ of soil, on a regular 0.25° grid (about 25 km), daily, 1991 to 2023. Top row: the satellite record on one day (a) and its uncertainty (b). Large areas are empty: the hatching marks where ERA5 soil temperature is below 0 °C, because microwave sensors cannot retrieve liquid soil water from frozen ground. Apart from missing satellite overpasses in the early years, the paper ranks retrieval failures: “The most common cause are frozen soils”, then “dense vegetation, which can mask contributions from the soil” (tropical rainforests are masked permanently in the original record), then radio-frequency interference and very dry barren soils. The filled values compare with in situ stations about as well as the originals (global median R = 0.72, unbiased RMSD 0.05 m³/m³, paper’s evaluation). Bottom row: the gap-filled product (c) and its uncertainty (d). Most gaps are filled with a statistical method (discrete cosine transform with penalized least squares) that borrows from neighboring days and cells; the long frozen-soil gaps, the hatched areas here, are filled mainly by linear interpolation in time between the previous autumn and the following spring. Ice sheets stay excluded. Two discussion points. The gaps are not random: they follow temperature, vegetation and orbit, which is the informative missingness of 2.4 on a grid. And a filled value is an estimate, not a measurement; keep the flag that says which cells were filled, or a model trained on the product will learn the filling method as if it were soil physics.

Satellite: the local time each orbit samples drifts

🛰️ Satellite & airborne 🌊 Ocean 🌊 Oceanography

Embury et al. (2024), Scientific Data 11, 326, Fig. 1, doi:10.1038/s41597-024-03147-w. CC BY 4.0.

NOAA-14 drifted from 13:40 local time in 1995 to after 18:00 by 2002; the record adjusts every observation to 10:30 or 22:30 so the daily warming cycle does not alias into the trend.

Three minutes; this connects to the tide-gauge aliasing slide later today. Each line is one satellite carrying an infrared radiometer used for the ESA CCI sea surface temperature record since 1980 (N = NOAA platforms carrying AVHRR, the Advanced Very High Resolution Radiometer; AT = ATSR, the Along-Track Scanning Radiometer; M = MetOp; S3 = Sentinel-3 with SLSTR, the ATSR successor; AMSR-E and AMSR2 are microwave sensors). The vertical axis is the local solar time at which the satellite crosses the equator in daylight. A sun-synchronous orbit samples the ocean at the same local time every day, so it samples the daily warming cycle at one phase only, like keeping one tide-gauge reading a day. The orbits drift: NOAA-14 crossed near 13:40 in 1995 and after 18:00 by 2002. Sea surface temperature warms by up to 0.5 K during a typical day, and by more than 5 K on calm sunny days (paper’s text), so a drifting sampling time turns the daily cycle into a spurious slow trend. The product’s fix is to adjust every observation to the nearest 10:30 or 22:30 local mean solar time. The gridded product: 0.05° (about 5 km) daily, with gaps (level 3) and gap-free (level 4).

Keep one sample a day — and the tide invents a fortnight

0.59 m RMS error — keep each midnight reading

0.02 m RMS error — average the day first, then keep one value

The M2 tide (12.42-h period) lives above a daily series’ Nyquist frequency. Subsampled, it does not vanish — it folds into a 14.8-day oscillation at nearly full tidal amplitude.

Thirty times worse — and the artifact masquerades as a plausible fortnightly ocean signal.

Set up the task: an hourly tide gauge, and a scientist who wants a daily series to study slow sea-level variability. The lazy move — keep the midnight sample — does not just lose the tide, it transforms it: sampling at 1 cycle/day can only represent frequencies below 0.5 cycles/day (define Nyquist here, from the literature slide), and M2 at 1.93 cycles/day folds to |1.93 − 2| = 0.068 cycles/day — a 14.8-day period. Fortnightly tidal modulation is a real oceanographic phenomenon, which is exactly why the artifact is dangerous: it is physically dressed. Numbers are the executed notebook’s, graded against the generator’s tide-free truth. The rule, stated once and repeated: before reducing a sampling rate, ask what lives above the new Nyquist and remove it first.

Three ways to make a daily series

The daily mean — a humble 24-h average — already suppresses the tide; a proper anti-alias filter does slightly better. The midnight samples invent a half-meter oscillation that was never in the ocean.

Walk the curves: red = midnight subsampling, swinging ±0.8 m at the folded 14.8-day period; orange = daily mean (a 24-hour boxcar is a crude low-pass filter — that is why the everyday resample-to-daily-mean idiom is largely safe); blue = decimation with a designed anti-alias filter; black dashed = the true subtidal sea level, known because the data are synthetic (mlgeo_synth) with the tide returned separately. Real-world anchor for the notes: satellite altimetry lives with this permanently — the TOPEX/Jason orbit revisits each point every ~10 days, aliasing M2 into a ~62-day signal that must be modeled away; mission orbits are designed around tidal aliasing. Gaps: interpolation is a decision, not a default

Synthetic GNSS with planted truth (mlgeo_synth): a 150-day outage hides a 25 mm earthquake step — interpolation draws a confident straight ramp through it. Policy: fill gaps ≤ 10 days (error below the 2.4 mm noise), mask the rest.

The scenario: a daily GNSS record degraded with eight random short gaps plus one 150-day outage placed across an earthquake — a station knocked out by the shaking it was supposed to record is not hypothetical. Gray = truth (never observed), red dashed = what linear interpolation fabricates, blue = the stated policy. The graded numbers: short gaps interpolate at 0.7–2.4 mm RMS — at or below the 2.4 mm daily noise, because trend plus seasonal cycle barely move in a few days; the 150-day gap costs 8.1 mm RMS and, worse, the filled values are confidently wrong — smooth, plausible, and treated as measurements by any downstream trend fit or ML feature window. The policy is the lesson: a threshold derived from this station’s signal rates (faster station → shorter threshold), written in the data card; NaN is an honest statement. Mantra from the notebook: fix what the data constrain, mask what they do not, write the threshold down.

Three minutes; this extends the previous slide from one station to the methods in use. Row by row, with sources (all in figs/lit_gap_filling/SOURCES.md). Leave missing: ESA CCI sea surface temperature keeps the cloud gaps in its level 2 and 3 products and gives each value a 0 to 5 quality level; only the level 4 analysis is gap-free, and the authors write that “the process of interpolation inevitably means feature resolution is degraded”, so users studying fronts and eddies should use the gappy data (Merchant et al. 2019). HadCRUT4 left cells without data empty; as Cowtan and Way point out, leaving a cell out of an area-weighted mean is the same as filling it with the mean of the observed cells, next slide. Drop rows: dropping is safe only if the gaps are missing completely at random (MCAR); Rubin (1976) shows that likelihood methods can ignore the gap mechanism under the weaker missing-at-random condition, but row deletion cannot; this is notebook 2.4’s MCAR and MNAR. Interpolate: NOAA’s daily OISST grids satellite, ship and buoy SST by optimum interpolation; HadCRUT5’s analysis variant fills gaps with a Gaussian-process method close to kriging, and ships the unfilled grid next to it (Morice et al. 2021). Statistical reconstruction: DINEOF fills gaps from the leading EOFs (empirical orthogonal functions, the dominant spatial patterns) (Beckers and Rixen 2003); ESA CCI soil moisture uses a DCT-PLS smoother (discrete cosine transform, penalized least squares) and fills long frozen-soil gaps by linear interpolation in time (Preimesberger et al. 2025); flux towers use marginal distribution sampling, a look-up of measurements made under similar weather (Reichstein et al. 2005). Model or reanalysis: FLUXNET fills tower weather from ERA-Interim downscaled to each site; the correction removes only 10 to 36% of the mismatch and wind speed is filled poorly (Vuichard and Papale 2015). ML: DINCAE, a convolutional autoencoder for satellite SST and altimetry (Barth et al. 2020, 2022); XGBoost for flux towers (Vekuri et al. 2023); AutoML to bridge the 11-month gap between GRACE and GRACE-FO (Sun et al. 2021); for the same gap, a benchmark without neural networks found a simple fit of trend plus annual and semi-annual terms scores well, and at one point gap filling underestimated the annual power by 20% (Lecomte et al. 2024). Notebook 2.6 section 3.2b tries three of these rows (linear, a fitted model with and without the catalogued earthquake step, gradient-boosted trees) on the synthetic GNSS outage and keeps a filled flag. The flags: FLUXNET2015 marks every meteorological value 0 measured, 1 filled from similar weather, 2 filled from reanalysis, and every filled flux 0 measured, 1 to 3 filled with high, medium or low confidence (Pastorello et al. 2020); ESA CCI soil moisture ships a filled/observed mask and a per-value uncertainty.

The cause: northern radiation distributions are skewed, so similar-weather look-ups pick the wrong neighbours.

Three minutes; this is the lecture’s link to the evaluation thread of chapters 3 and 6. The figure: DINCAE 2.0, a convolutional autoencoder, reconstructing Mediterranean sea level anomaly (m) on 7 June 2017 from along-track satellite altimetry: (a) reconstruction, (b) its own error estimate, (c) the tracks used for training over a 21-day window, (d) tracks withheld for validation. Whole tracks go to one split only (70% train, 20% development, 10% test), so neighbouring points on a track never sit on both sides (Barth et al. 2022). Each bullet with its source. Real gap shapes: DINCAE 1.0 imposed the cloud masks of the first 50 images on the last 50 (Barth et al. 2020); Vekuri et al. 2023 drew artificial gaps from the real gap lengths and times. Own input versus hidden pixels: on one Adriatic MODIS image, DINCAE 2.0 differs from the data it was given by 0.15 °C RMS and from pixels hidden under added clouds by 0.55 °C (Barth et al. 2022, Fig. 5). Tuned baseline: on the test tracks, DINCAE 3.58 cm against 3.60 cm for DIVAnd, a variational interpolator tuned by Bayesian optimization; with SST as a second input, DINCAE reached 3.47 cm; the authors say the main gain is a more reliable error estimate. Places, not pixels: a random-forest biomass map in central Africa scored R² = 0.53 with random 10-fold cross-validation and 0.14 with spatial blocks, close to a model with no predictors (Ploton et al. 2020). Filled values on their own: in one ESA CCI soil moisture example area with 40% missing data, the anomaly correlation with in situ stations is 0.59 for filled values and 0.70 for observed ones (Preimesberger et al. 2025). Also check the error estimate itself: DINCAE 1.0’s normalized errors had a standard deviation of 0.85, so it overestimated its error by about 15%. Notebook 2.6 section 3.2c makes the same point on the GNSS series: hidden away from the earthquake in blocks of 1 to 150 days, linear and boosted-tree fillers both score 1.6 to 3.1 mm; on the real 150-day outage, which hides the earthquake, they score 8.1 and 21.7 mm. A trajectory model with a step at the catalogued earthquake date scores 2.8 mm there: a model fills well when it contains the process.

The irregular stream: 25 wells, 40 years, no grid

Averaging raw heads jumps by meters whenever a well enters or leaves the record. Remove each well’s own level first, weight by measurement quality — and the true regional decline emerges: RMS 2.42 → 1.42 m.

The third stream from lecture 4, finally in action: 2,902 visits across 25 wells, 1981–2019, every well with its own datum offset (meters!), noise level, active period, and visit schedule — operational hydrology as it actually looks. Top panel: the naive quarterly mean of raw heads; its meter-scale jumps are wells with high offsets entering and leaving — the curve is a history of which wells were visited, not of the aquifer. Bottom: two moves fix most of it — per-well anomalies (subtract each well’s own mean, so offsets cancel; Hansen & Lebedeff’s temperature method) and inverse-variance weights (1/σ², so a steel-tape reading cannot dilute a transducer record). Nothing sophisticated: the improvement comes from refusing to average incompatible things. Residual error is mostly each well sampling a different segment of the 40-year decline. Error bars for noise with memory

±0.019 mm/yr velocity uncertainty — resample individual days

±0.148 mm/yr velocity uncertainty — resample 100-day blocks

GNSS noise is time-correlated. Resampling single days destroys that memory: the true velocity sat 8.6σ outside the narrow bar — and 1.1σ inside the honest one.

The wider error bar is the correct one. The narrow bar was not conservative — it was wrong, and only the planted truth exposed it.

Close the loop on the bootstrap thread. Section 2 of the notebook fit a 12 mm/yr synthetic GNSS trend and bootstrapped the slope by resampling (day, displacement) pairs: a beautifully tight histogram — with the true velocity 8.6 standard deviations outside it. Diagnosis: pair resampling treats residuals as independent, but flicker and random-walk noise have memory, and correlated noise is precisely what makes a trend uncertain. Fix (Künsch, literature slide): resample contiguous 100-day blocks of residuals so correlation survives up to the block length — the bar widens by roughly a factor of seven and the truth lands ~1σ from the estimate, which is what honesty looks like. Block length is itself a stated decision (longer than the noise correlation time — exercise 4 probes sensitivity). Sting for the room: on real data there is no planted truth to flag the narrow bar — Mao et al. is a whole field learning this in print. The same caveat applies to the real P395 velocity fit in the notebook.
