Literature example: validating a forest biomass map

🛰️ Satellite & airborne 🌱 Biosphere 🦠 Biogeosciences

One random-forest map of central African forest biomass, scored two ways: R² = 0.53 when test plots are drawn at random, 0.14 when whole regions are held out, and close to zero beyond 100 km from the training plots.

Ploton, P., et al. (2020). Spatial validation reveals poor predictive performance of large-scale ecological mapping models. Nature Communications, 11, 4540. Figure 4, CC BY 4.0.

Inputs: satellite reflectance and climate layers. Target: field-measured aboveground biomass (Mg/ha). Same model, same data; only the partition changed.

(a) random 10-fold cross-validation; (b) spatial 44-fold cross-validation. Ploton et al. (2020), Fig. 4, CC BY 4.0.

A random split of spatially correlated plots reported R² = 0.53; holding out regions gave 0.14, the skill the map actually has away from field plots.

Four minutes; the paper told in full. Geoscience first: forest biomass maps of central Africa feed carbon budgets and conservation decisions. They are built by training a model on field plots (trees measured and converted to aboveground biomass) and predicting everywhere from satellite reflectance and climate layers. Ploton et al. (2020) took one such random-forest map and validated it twice. Random 10-fold cross-validation, the usual practice, gives R² = 0.53 (RMSPE 56.5 Mg/ha, 19%). Spatial 44-fold cross-validation, holding out whole regions, gives R² = 0.14 (RMSPE 77.5 Mg/ha, 26%). With an exclusion buffer, R² falls to about 0.15 at 50 km from training plots and is virtually null beyond 100 km (their Fig. 5). Nearby plots share soils, climate and forest type, so a random split tests the model on near-copies of its training data. This is criterion B7 in one figure, and the reason the rubric asks which axis the test set lies outside. Numbers checked against the article text; figure reused under CC BY 4.0.

What an AI-ready dataset looks like: INSTANCE

🗂️ Catalog & compiled product 🪨 Solid Earth 📈 Seismology

Magnitude, epicentral distance, depth, back azimuth of about 1.2 million traces from about 50,000 Italian earthquakes. Michelini et al. (2021), ESSD, 13, 5509–5544, Fig. 3, CC BY 4.0.

The step at M 2 is deliberate: the authors kept most M ≥ 2 events and added 20,000 random M < 2 events to counter the Gutenberg–Richter imbalance.

Two minutes. INSTANCE, the Italian seismic dataset for machine learning, is a good picture of a dataset described for learning. One sample is a 3-component trace, 120 s at 100 Hz, shipped both in counts and in ground-motion units (A2 homogenization), with more than 100 source, station and trace parameters (A4), from 620 stations (B1), plus 132,288 noise traces (B3; signal-to-noise peaks near 10 dB on the horizontal components and 6 dB on the vertical, their Fig. 10). Panel (a) is criterion B2: the authors reshaped the magnitude distribution on purpose, and say so. Panels (b) to (d) show what a model trained on it has and has not seen: few traces beyond 400 km, two back-azimuth peaks from the geometry of Italian seismicity. What it does not ship is a partition (B7), which every user then has to design. Numbers read from the paper (Sect. 2.1.1, 2.3, 3.1).

Analysis-ready and AI-ready

Analysis-ready

A scientist can compute with the data without redoing the provider’s work.

  • provenance, version, license
  • homogenized: one unit system, reference frame, time base
  • quality flags, gaps, and censoring documented
  • standard format with readable metadata

AI-ready

The data also support training a model and testing whether it generalizes.

  • enough independent samples, diverse and with a known distribution
  • noise characterized: instrument versus natural variability
  • labels with known quality, or structure for self-supervision
  • can be partitioned into within-domain and out-of-domain sets

Analysis readiness is necessary for AI readiness but not sufficient: a clean, well-documented record from one station in one decade can still fail every generalization test.

Two minutes. Analysis-ready is the older idea: satellite agencies define analysis-ready data as products that need no further processing before use (CEOS CARD4L), and the analysis-ready, cloud-optimized movement (Abernathey et al. 2021) applied it to large gridded archives. AI-ready adds requirements that only matter when a model will learn from the data and be judged on held-out data. Use the GNSS example: P395 is analysis-ready (NGL homogenizes the processing, flags steps, publishes the file), but one station cannot test whether a model trained on Cascadia generalizes to the San Andreas.

AI readiness: what the samples contain

Criterion Question Geoscience example
B1 Diversity how many independent units: stations, events, regions, years? 1.8 million QTM earthquakes, but one region and one network (Session 1)
B2 Distribution how are targets and inputs distributed; how rare is the class of interest? flood days are 1.3% to 6.3% of days at the four 2.13 gauges; granite to andesite 5.6 : 1; magnitudes follow a power law
B3 Noise which variability is the instrument and which is signal for another question? ocean microseism is noise for earthquake detection and signal for wave climate (Session 9)

Count independent units, not rows: 99% of the 36,518 Pacific Northwest earthquakes in Session 5 came from one network, so they say little about other networks.

Three minutes. B1: rows are cheap, independence is not. Daily samples from one gauge are correlated over weeks, events in one aftershock sequence share a source, pixels in one scene share an illumination. The number that matters for generalization is the number of independent units. B2: the distribution of the training data sets what the model learns to predict well; a power-law magnitude distribution means very few large events, which are often the ones that matter. B3: Session 9’s point returns: noise is defined by the question. Characterize the instrumental noise (sensor self-noise, processing scatter such as GNSS’s 1.8 mm daily scatter) separately from natural variability that another question would call signal.

AI readiness: what a model can learn from

Criterion Question Geoscience example
B4 Augmentation which transformations keep the physics? adding recorded noise to earthquake waveforms keeps the physics; time-reversing a flood hydrograph does not
B5 Labels who labeled, how consistently; or can structure replace labels? analyst phase picks vary in quality; years of unlabeled continuous waveforms allow self-supervised training
B6 Dimensionality samples versus features; input shape; intrinsic dimension STEAD: 3 components × 6,000 samples; first PCA mode of a temperature field: 95.5% of variance (2.12)

An augmentation is valid only if the instrument could have recorded the transformed sample.

Three minutes. B4: the test for an augmentation is physical: could the instrument have recorded the transformed sample? Shifting a waveform window, adding recorded noise at the measured level, rotating horizontal components of a seismogram, synthetic events with known truth (Session 9) pass. Time reversal of a causal process, mirroring an image whose shadows encode sun azimuth, or adding white noise to data whose noise is red do not. B5: labels in geoscience are often analyst decisions (phase picks, landslide outlines, IPCC confidence levels in Session 3); their consistency bounds the score any model can reach. Where labels are scarce, abundant unlabeled data with structure (continuous waveforms, satellite archives) support self-supervised learning. B6: the number of features against the number of independent samples, the shape of each input, and the intrinsic dimension from PCA (2.12).

B7: partitioning into within-domain and out-of-domain sets

Training and validation come from the same domain; the test set lies outside it along a stated axis: statistical, spatiotemporal, or physical.

Four minutes; this is the slide to slow down on. Left: statistical. In the space of input features, measure the distance of each new sample to the training set (here the nearest-neighbor distance, standardized with training statistics only). Samples farther than the training points are from each other are outside the domain, whatever their date or place; Meyer and Pebesma (2021) call the inside the area of applicability. Middle: spatiotemporal. Validation years are drawn at the training gauges; the test set is a later period and a gauge the model never saw. Right: physical. The test floods are larger than any flood in training: a different event size, and possibly different physics (overbank flow, dam operations). Ask: for your project, which axis matters most to the people who would use the model?

What each partition tests

Partition Drawn from Tests Used for
Training the domain the model can fit the relationships fitting parameters
Validation the same domain, independent units the model interpolates within the domain choosing hyperparameters, stopping training
Test, statistical far from training in feature space extrapolation in the inputs reporting generalization
Test, spatiotemporal a later period or a new region transfer in space or time reporting generalization
Test, physical a different process, event size, or physics transfer to conditions not sampled reporting generalization

In notebook 2.13 one flood classifier scores 0.74 within the domain, 0.70 on later years, and 0.20 at a gauge it never saw (average precision).

Two minutes. Machine-learning models are good interpolators: the validation score tells you the model learned the training domain. Only an out-of-domain test tells you whether it generalizes, and only along the axis you held out, so state the axis. The notebook makes this concrete with four synthetic Washington gauges (coastal rain, snowmelt, mixed, semi-arid flash floods) and one random forest. Average precision is 0.740 on held-out months at the three training gauges and 0.736 on a random split: here a random split is barely more flattering. It drops to 0.703 on 2022 to 2024 and to 0.200 at the semi-arid gauge G4, which the model never saw. 36% of G4 samples lie beyond the 95th-percentile distance from the training set, against 6% of validation samples. Floods larger than the 80th percentile of training floods are estimated at a median 0.69 of their observed peak. A random split of strongly autocorrelated data (daily samples within one flood) can still put near-copies of training samples in the test set; the notebook’s cross-validation comparison at gauge G1 shows it: KFold 0.730, GroupKFold 0.707, TimeSeriesSplit 0.640. A published example of a partition error changing a conclusion: Frame et al. (2022, HESS, doi:10.5194/hess-26-3377-2022) tested a deep-learning runoff model on extreme floods; their 2023 corrigendum found a date error in the train/test split, and once corrected, the calibrated National Water Model beat the LSTM on events with return periods of 25 years or more. Connect forward: the week 7 robust-training session (3.8) builds the full validation ladder on these ideas.
