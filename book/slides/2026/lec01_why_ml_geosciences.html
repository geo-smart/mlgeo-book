Literature example: Southern California earthquakes

🗂️ Catalog 🪨 Solid Earth 📈 Seismology

Template matching on ten years (2008–2017) of Southern California Seismic Network waveforms found 1.81 million earthquakes, ten times the network’s catalog, and lowered the magnitude of completeness from 1.7 to 0.3.

Ross, Trugman, Hauksson & Shearer (2019). Science, 364, 767–771.

Four minutes; this is the one paper the lecture tells in full, and it is in our home discipline. Geoscience first: the map is Southern California, the Pacific–North America plate boundary (dashed, PB2002), and the Southern California Seismic Network (SCSN) catalog, which analysts build by picking P and S arrivals on events large enough to see. Ross, Trugman, Hauksson and Shearer (2019, Science 364, 767–771, doi:10.1126/science.aaw6888) took the 284,149 cataloged earthquakes of 2000–2017 as templates and cross-correlated their waveforms against every day of continuous data from 2008 to 2017 on 513 stations (supplement). A new event is declared when the correlation, stacked over stations and phases, exceeds 9.5 times its median absolute deviation (MAD), and it inherits the location of its template before relocation. The result is the Quake Template Matching (QTM) catalog: 1,811,362 events, a tenfold increase, and a magnitude of completeness of 0.3 against 1.7 for the SCSN catalog over the same years (the paper’s goodness-of-fit estimates, supplement Fig. S5; the dashed lines on the plot). Then the table: each data sample is one event, with a time, a hypocenter, a magnitude, and the metadata that say how it was found: which template detected it and how well it correlated (CC). The bold row is SCSN event 10297553, M0.82; the three rows above it are foreshocks within 16 seconds, at magnitudes the network catalog cannot see, found by that event’s own waveforms. Where the result shows: the frequency–magnitude plot (the two catalogs agree above M 2 and QTM keeps going down to about M 0), and the daily counts (QTM sits a factor of ten above SCSN every day, including the aftershocks of the 2010 M7.2 El Mayor–Cucapah earthquake). Our rerun from the SCEDC files today reproduces the published count exactly (1,811,362) and gives 180,069 SCSN earthquakes for 2008–2017 (quarry blasts removed), a ratio of 10.06; median 410 events per day in QTM against 39. What the scientists did, in the spirit of today’s question: they chose the threshold by inspecting thousands of detections, estimated a false-alarm rate of about 5% at 9.5 MAD (under 1% at 12 MAD, the 853,764-event high-confidence version), and say on the data page that the catalog is fully automated and only partly reviewed. Two honest caveats for the room: template matching is cross-correlation on 200 GPUs, not a trained model; deep-learning phase pickers (PhaseNet, EQTransformer, 2019–2020) later made catalogs of this density routine, and lecture 22 builds a small detector of that kind. Everything on the slide is regenerated by figscripts/qtm_hidden_earthquakes.py from https://service.scedc.caltech.edu/ftp/QTMcatalog/qtm_final_9.5dev.hypo and the yearly SCSN files at https://service.scedc.caltech.edu/ftp/catalogs/SCEC_DC/. Machine learning results in other Earth systems

90% of 1,380

Atmosphere. A graph neural network trained on reanalysis outperforms the best operational deterministic forecast on 90% of 1,380 verification targets; a 10-day forecast takes under a minute.

Lam, R., et al. (2023). Learning skillful medium-range global weather forecasting. Science, 382, 1416–1421.

1.5 years

Ocean. A convolutional network trained on climate simulations, then on reanalysis, forecasts El Niño with skill up to one and a half years ahead.

Ham, Y.-G., Kim, J.-H., & Luo, J.-J. (2019). Deep learning for multi-year ENSO forecasts. Nature, 573, 568–572.

5 days

Land water. In ungauged watersheds, a model trained on gauged ones predicts extreme floods 5 days ahead as reliably as the global flood system’s same-day nowcast.

Nearing, G., et al. (2024). Global prediction of extreme floods in ungauged watersheds. Nature, 627, 559–563.

2 vs 13,451

Solid Earth. A logistic regression with 2 parameters matches the aftershock-location skill of a deep network with 13,451.

Mignan, A., & Broccardo, M. (2019). One neuron versus deep learning in aftershock prediction. Nature, 574, E1–E3.

Reading-arc anchors: Bergen et al. (2019, Science) on solid-Earth geoscience; Karpatne et al. (2019, IEEE TKDE) on why geoscience data resist off-the-shelf methods.

Three forecasts match or beat physics-based systems; in the fourth case a 2-parameter regression matched a 13,451-parameter network.

Two minutes, one sentence per card, then point at the evaluation behind each number. Lam et al. (2023, Science, doi:10.1126/science.adi2336): GraphCast, trained on ECMWF’s ERA5 reanalysis, outperforms ECMWF’s high-resolution deterministic forecast (HRES) on 90% of 1,380 verification targets and produces a 10-day, 0.25° global forecast in under a minute on one TPU; ECMWF now runs its own machine-learned forecast alongside the physics model. Ham, Kim and Luo (2019, Nature, doi:10.1038/s41586-019-1559-7): too few observed El Niño events to train on, so the network learns first from CMIP5 historical simulations and then from reanalysis for 1871–1973, and is validated on 1984–2017, where its all-season correlation skill for the Niño3.4 index stays useful to about 1.5 years, above the dynamical forecast systems. Nearing et al. (2024, Nature, doi:10.1038/s41586-024-07145-1): an LSTM trained on gauged rivers forecasts extreme floods in ungauged watersheds with 5-day-ahead reliability similar to or better than the same-day nowcasts of the Copernicus Global Flood Awareness System (GloFAS), and now runs operationally with free forecasts in over 80 countries; the ungauged test is a leave-locations-out evaluation, the design of section 3.8 (lecture 18). Mignan and Broccardo (2019, Nature, doi:10.1038/s41586-019-1582-8) is the caution: DeVries et al. (2018, Nature) predicted aftershock locations with a six-layer network of 13,451 parameters; a logistic regression with 2 parameters reaches the same area under the curve (about 0.85), so the network added no predictive skill over a simple baseline. Kapoor and Narayanan (2023, Patterns, doi:10.1016/j.patter.2023.100804) document the same kind of overoptimism, mostly from data leakage, across 17 fields; mention it if a student asks whether this is specific to geoscience. The dim line names the two survey papers that open the reading arc: Bergen, Johnson, de Hoop and Beroza (2019, Science 363, eaau0323) and Karpatne, Ebert-Uphoff, Ravela, Babaie and Kumar (2019, IEEE TKDE 31, 1544–1554). Daily positions at GNSS stations P395 and P563

📍 In-situ sensor 🪨 Solid Earth 📐 Geodesy

Each point is the station’s daily east position, measured to a few millimeters. In this global frame both stations drift west, and 12 to 13 mm/yr of that drift is the North America plate itself. What remains is deformation at the plate boundary (map arrows, relative to stable North America): the Oregon coast at P395 is pushed landward while the Cascadia megathrust is locked, and P563 is carried northwest in the shear zone between the Pacific and North America plates.

P563 drifts west at 23.5 mm/yr in the global frame; 11.6 mm/yr of that is the North America plate.

This is the course’s first data set; notebook 1.7 downloads it live and it returns in Chapters 2 and 3. All rates are NGL MIDAS (IGS20 solution), retrieved 2026-10-05. Read the time series first: it is the east position in the global IGS20 frame. P395 moves east at −6.4 mm/yr and P563 at −23.5 mm/yr in that frame. Relative to stable North America the same stations move +6.9 and −11.9 mm/yr east, so the plate’s own motion accounts for 13.3 and 11.6 mm/yr of westward drift; that is the meaning of “reference frame”, which session 3 returns to. At P395 the remaining northeast motion is commonly attributed to elastic loading of the upper plate while the subduction interface is locked, with a contribution from rotation of the Oregon forearc (McCaffrey et al., 2013). At P563, about half of the 2.3 cm/yr westward drift is the plate and half is shear across the Pacific–North America boundary zone; the station also subsides at 7.2 mm/yr, a rate consistent with groundwater extraction in the San Joaquin Valley. The annual oscillations at both stations are seasonal signals such as hydrological loading. P395 has 7,496 daily solutions in the course file as of Oct 7, 2026 (the file grows daily). Ask the room: what could you extract from this? (Velocities, seasonal loading, earthquake offsets, slow slip.) Open-science point: this exists because NGL publishes it freely; cite Blewitt et al. (2018, Eos). Data citation is part of section 1.1.

P395 east position: the record and a synthetic copy

📍 In-situ sensor 🪨 Solid Earth 📐 Geodesy

One of Series 1 and 2 is the station; the other is NGL’s model plus independent random noise of the same size. Which is synthetic, and what gives it away?

Series 1 is synthetic. The real record wanders for weeks at a time, and its 2016 drop starts in January, months before the step the model places at the antenna change.

Three minutes: one minute alone, one with a neighbor, one to collect answers. The top panel is NGL’s trajectory model for P395 (east component): a constant rate of −6.4 mm/yr, annual and semi-annual sinusoids, and four steps, all read from NGL’s .modfit file; it leaves a 1.8 mm RMS misfit, the value NGL reports. Series 1 adds independent Gaussian noise with that RMS to the model. Most students identify the synthetic from texture: its scatter is the same every day, while real errors are correlated in time. The second clue is the 2016 drop: the model explains it with steps at the equipment changes on 2016-05-12 and 2016-06-10, but the observed decline begins around January. Do not resolve that yet; it is feature D on the next slide.

P395’s residual holds 1.8 mm RMS daily scatter, equipment steps of +3.0 and −5.9 mm, and months-long wander that the synthetic series lacks.

Reveal after the votes. A: NGL’s residual RMS for this fit is 1.83 mm east. B, C, D: the catalogue records equipment changes (code 1) and earthquakes whose epicenter falls within a magnitude-dependent distance of the station (code 2); NGL notes that a flagged earthquake may cause no displacement, and C is such a case: the model includes the step and estimates it as zero within uncertainty. D is the open case: equipment changes explain a jump, but the observed decline starts in early 2016, so part of it may be ground motion or seasonal signal that the constant-amplitude sinusoids miss. E: the synthetic residual shows what independent errors look like; the real residual’s months-long excursions mean the errors are correlated or the model is missing physics, and one station cannot separate the two. Neighboring stations can: motion shared across a network is either common-mode error or regional physics.

Four uses of machine learning in the geosciences

  • Automation: detect and catalog events in continuous records
  • Discovery: find structure nobody labeled, in data too large to read whole
  • Emulation: replace an expensive physical simulation with a fast surrogate
  • Forecasting: predict the next value, with its uncertainty

Signal processing (denoising, gap filling) is a fifth use, grouped here with automation. Matching the method to the question is learning outcome 1.

Today’s papers cover three uses: detection (QTM, 10 times the cataloged events), emulation (GraphCast, a 10-day forecast in under a minute), forecasting (floods 5 days ahead).

Give one concrete example per line. Automation: deep-learning phase pickers now build earthquake catalogs with many times more events than analyst catalogs. Discovery: clustering found new families of repeating signals in continuous seismic data and unexpected regimes in ocean color. Emulation: ML surrogates for ground-motion simulation and ocean models run in milliseconds instead of node-hours. Forecasting: streamflow and shaking forecasts, where the uncertainty statement matters as much as the number. The pattern across all four: the geoscience question comes first, the method second, the ordering this course follows on every slide. The two literature slides at the start supply one published case for three of the four uses; discovery is the one without a card, and clustering (3.3, lecture 13) is where the course treats it. Earlier versions of this deck also showed AlphaFold (Jumper et al., 2021, Nature 596, 583–589) and GNoME materials discovery (Merchant et al., 2023, Nature 624, 80–85); they were cut because they are not geoscience results, but AlphaFold is a fair answer if a student asks for the clearest case of machine learning solving a long-open scientific problem.

Open, reproducible science: the course standard

  • Reproducible: your code + your data give your numbers, on someone else’s machine
  • Replicable: same code, new data, same conclusion
  • This course grades the first and aims for the second
  • Everything behind a figure: committed, licensed, citable, rerunnable

Today’s data are free to download (NGL positions, the SCEDC catalogs); your repository must be equally rerunnable, with a license and a citable DOI.

This is section 1.1, today’s reading. Define the FAIR principles aloud, one line each from 1.1: findable (a DOI and rich metadata, indexed somewhere searchable), accessible (retrievable by a standard protocol such as HTTPS; “available from the author on request” fails), interoperable (open, documented formats), reusable (a clear license and enough provenance). Then the two practical habits: a license file in every repository (“public” does not mean “licensed” — without a license nobody may legally reuse your work; a repository that holds both code and data gets two licenses, MIT for the code and CC-BY for the data, with the split stated in the README) and a DOI for anything citable (Zenodo mints them free from GitHub releases). Then the AI-era point from 1.1: you might think regenerable code makes archiving obsolete; it does the opposite. AI-generated code is not deterministic; the committed code in the locked environment is the only record of what actually ran. Verification is the new bottleneck, and the reproducible artifact is what makes verification possible.

Fair evaluation across the quarter

  • Design the evaluation before the model: baseline, data design, metric
  • Week 7, twice: honest validation for models (3.8), then for AI agents (6.3)
  • Leaderboards score you on hidden test data, the way deployment does
  • Capstone: your own review agent, tested against ground truth

The same evaluation design (baseline, held-out data, metric) is applied to your models in 3.8 and to AI agents in 6.3.

This is the spine of the course, worth naming on day one. The story students will live: in week 7 they watch a model’s score collapse when validation days move from shuffled-into-training to the future — same model, same data, different question. The following Friday they apply the identical discipline to an AI agent: task spec, eval set with ground truth, failure analysis. The aftershock card on the literature slide (Mignan & Broccardo, 2019) is what happens when a model is not compared with a simple baseline, and Kapoor & Narayanan (2023, Patterns) document leakage-driven overoptimism across 17 fields. Promise them: by December, “what was your baseline and what did your test set hold out?” will be the first question they ask of any paper, any leaderboard, any AI demo.
