Today’s question

Analysts at the Pacific Northwest Seismic Network labeled 4,000 events as earthquakes, explosions, surface events or noise.

If we hide the labels, do the waveforms still fall into those four groups?

Reading: 3.3 Clustering · uses PCA from 2.12

Read the question aloud and ask for a show of hands: who thinks the four groups come back? Most people say yes; the answer at the end of the slides is “partly”. Clustering is the first method in the course that never sees a label. Labels are used only afterwards, to judge what it found.

Literature example: water masses from Argo profiles

📍 In-situ sensor 🌊 Ocean 🌊 Oceanography

Maze et al. (2017), Progress in Oceanography 151, 275–292 · rebuilt from 2023 Argo profiles (Ifremer ERDDAP)

Grouping profiles by shape alone, with no positions given, recovers ocean regions such as the subpolar gyre and the Eighteen Degree Water.

Four minutes; the example told in full. The ocean: Argo floats drift at depth, rise every 10 days and measure temperature from about 2,000 m to the surface. Maze et al. (2017) asked whether the North Atlantic’s temperature profiles fall into a few types without being told where they come from. Method, the same as today’s notebook in a different ocean: one sample is a profile on 280 depth levels from 5 to 1,400 m; standardize each level; PCA keeps 11 components (99.88% of the variance); a Gaussian mixture groups the profiles; the number of classes, 8, is where the BIC (a score that rewards fit and penalizes extra parameters) is lowest. We rebuilt it from open data (Ifremer ERDDAP, 5,214 profiles from 574 floats, the first 10 days of each month of 2023) and got the same two choices: 11 components and a BIC minimum at 8. Point at the map: no latitude or longitude went into the clustering, yet each class occupies a region. Class 2 is the subpolar gyre (Labrador and Irminger Seas). Class 8 is the western subtropical gyre, with 18 °C water at 300 m: the Eighteen Degree Water mode water. Class 6 is warmest at 1,000 m relative to its neighbors: the salty Mediterranean water spreading west. Ask the room: why do the classes form regions even though position was not used? Answer: profiles from the same water mass look alike, and water masses occupy regions. Two differences with the paper, to say honestly: our rebuild has a Nordic Seas class (158 profiles) that the paper’s region did not separate, and the paper’s southeastern subtropical gyre class has no clear counterpart here; one year of data is not fifteen. The paper’s figures are not open-licensed, so the figure is ours (figscripts/argo_profile_clusters.py).

Clustering in other geoscience problems

46,000

Earthquake spectra from The Geysers, 2012 to 2014, grouped by k-means with no labels. Each of the four clusters peaks once a year, in turn, following the water-injection rate.

Holtzman, B. K., Paté, A., Paisley, J., Waldhauser, F., & Repetto, D. (2018). Machine learning reveals cyclic changes in seismic source spectra in Geysers geothermal field. Science Advances, 4(5), eaao2929.

10 → 4

A Gaussian mixture started with 10 clusters on one day of one Greenland station and kept 4. One of them holds the repeating precursors of the 2017 Nuugaatsiaq landslide.

Seydoux, L., Balestriero, R., Poli, P., de Hoop, M., Campillo, M., & Baraniuk, R. (2020). Clustering earthquake signals and background noises in continuous seismic data with unsupervised deep learning. Nature Communications, 11, 3972.

43%

K-means on the terms of the barotropic vorticity budget splits the global ocean into five main dynamical regimes. The largest, depth-coherent flow, covers 43% of the ocean area.

Sonnewald, M., Wunsch, C., & Heimbach, P. (2019). Unsupervised learning reveals geography of global ocean dynamical regions. Earth and Space Science, 6(5), 784–794.

k ≥ 4

On uniform noise, which has one cluster, all eight elbow-based rules tested pick four or more.

Schubert, E. (2023). Stop using the elbow criterion for k-means and how to choose the number of clusters instead. ACM SIGKDD Explorations Newsletter, 25(1), 36–42.

Clusters found without labels became findings when they lined up with something measured independently: an injection schedule, a landslide, a flow regime.

Two minutes, one sentence per card. Holtzman et al. (2018): 46,000 small earthquakes at The Geysers geothermal field, grouped by the shape of their spectra; each of four clusters peaks once a year, in turn, following the water-injection rate. Nobody gave the algorithm the injection data; the match is what makes the clusters believable. Seydoux et al. (2020): one day of continuous data at one Greenland station; a Gaussian mixture started with 10 groups kept 4, and one of them holds the repeating small signals before the 2017 Nuugaatsiaq landslide. Sonnewald et al. (2019): k-means on the terms of the ocean’s vorticity budget splits the global ocean into dynamical regimes; the largest covers 43% of the ocean area. Schubert (2023) is the warning that returns on the “choosing k” slide: on uniform noise, which has a single cluster, every elbow rule tested picks four or more.

Old Faithful: what is one sample?

🗂️ Catalog & compiled product 🪨 Solid Earth 🌋 Volcanology

One sample is a pair of consecutive eruptions, so two eruption modes make up to four kinds of pair; k-means finds them without labels (silhouette 0.56 at k = 2, 0.69 at k = 4).

Three minutes. Old Faithful in Yellowstone erupts in two modes: short (about 2 minutes) and long (about 4.5 minutes). Each point pairs one eruption’s duration with the next one’s, 271 pairs. Physics: a long eruption empties more of the reservoir, so recharge takes longer. Point at the left panel and ask the room: after a short eruption, what comes next? Answer: almost always a long one (94% in the notebook). After a long one? Either (52% short). Middle panel: k-means with k = 2 splits the pairs by the first eruption. Right panel: k = 4 finds the four kinds of pair, including the rare short-then-short group in the corner (6 pairs). The lesson: what counts as a group depends on what one sample is. The same data give two groups of eruptions and four groups of pairs. No labels and no physics were used, only distances between points.

Distance and the k-means loop

  • Every clustering method starts from a distance; k-means uses Euclidean distance between samples
  • Features with large numeric ranges dominate the distance, so standardize first
  • Guess k centers, assign each sample to the nearest, move each center to the mean of its samples, repeat
  • The loop stops at a local minimum, so run it from many starts and keep the best
km = KMeans(n_clusters=4, init="k-means++", n_init=20, random_state=42).fit(X)
km.labels_, km.cluster_centers_, km.inertia_   # group of each sample, centers, total squared distance

The groups depend on the distance you chose and on where the loop started, so both are part of the result.

Three minutes. Euclidean distance is the square root of the summed squared differences across features. Ask the room: if one feature is in meters and another in kilometers, which one decides the clusters? Answer: the one with the bigger numbers, which is why we standardize. Walk through the loop with your hands: drop k centers, everyone joins the nearest, centers move to the middle of their group, repeat until nothing changes. The quantity the loop lowers is the inertia, the sum of squared distances from each sample to its center. It can get stuck: in the notebook a bad start on Old Faithful gives inertia 183.9, while the best of 20 starts gives 74.0, the same as scikit-learn. That is why n_init=20. Students write two pieces of this loop themselves in the notebook (assign and update).

Choosing k: the elbow and the silhouette

With the regimes visible, inertia drops sharply up to k = 3 and the silhouette peaks at k = 4; blur the same pairs by 1.1 minutes and both curves flatten (silhouette 0.34 to 0.38).

Three minutes. Left: inertia divided by its value at k = 1. As recorded (blue), adding the second cluster removes 61% of the inertia, the third 25%, the fourth only 4.5%: a bend near k = 3. Right: the silhouette compares, for each sample, the mean distance to its own cluster (a) with the mean distance to the nearest other cluster (b): (b − a) / max(a, b), from −1 to 1. It peaks at k = 4 (0.69). So the elbow and the silhouette do not even agree here. Middle: the notebook’s stress test adds random scatter of one standardized unit (1.14 min) to every pair. The red curves bend gradually and the silhouette is flat: nothing in either curve says “there is no clear k”. Ask the room: which k would you report for the blurred data? There is no good answer, which is the point. Schubert (2023) argues against the elbow for this reason. Use these curves as guides and check against the physics.

PCA before clustering

  • One seismic event is described by 61 waveform features: durations, amplitude ratios, frequency content, spectral shape
  • Distances in 61 dimensions are dominated by many correlated features, so first reduce: PCA on the standardized features
  • 15 components keep 80.9% of the variance; the first alone keeps 26.5%
Z = StandardScaler().fit_transform(X_seis)          # 4,000 events x 61 features
pca = PCA().fit(Z)
n_pcs = np.searchsorted(np.cumsum(pca.explained_variance_ratio_), 0.80) + 1   # 15
X_pca = PCA(n_components=n_pcs).fit_transform(Z)

The seismic table has 61 features but about 15 directions that carry most of the variance; k-means runs on those 15.

Two minutes; this is a recap of notebook 2.12, which was self-study. Ask the room: why not cluster on all 61 features directly? Answer: many features measure nearly the same thing, so they count several times in the distance, and the space is mostly empty. PCA rotates the standardized features into uncorrelated directions ordered by variance, and we keep the fewest that reach 80%. This is rubric criterion B6 (dimensionality) from Session 10. In the notebook the result without PCA turns out almost the same (V-measure 0.375 vs 0.371), which is worth saying: PCA here speeds things up and removes redundancy, it does not change the answer much.

Pacific Northwest seismic sources without labels

📍 In-situ sensor 🪨 Solid Earth 📈 Seismology

Earthquakes get one cluster and noise two; explosions and surface events share one (730 and 777 events). V-measure: 0.37.

Four minutes; this is the answer to today’s question. Data: 4,000 events from the Pacific Northwest Seismic Network, 1,000 of each type, labeled by analysts (Kharita et al. 2026, Seismica). Surface events are rockfalls, debris flows and avalanches, many on Mount Rainier and Mount Hood. Explosions are mostly quarry and mine blasts. Pipeline: standardize, 15 principal components, k-means with k = 4, then compare clusters with the hidden labels. Read the table row by row with the room. Cluster 0 is mostly earthquakes, cluster 1 mostly noise, cluster 2 mixes explosions and surface events, cluster 3 is mostly noise. Ask: why would explosions and surface events look alike? Answer: both happen at the surface, close to the stations, without the deep, impulsive high-frequency energy of earthquakes, so their waveform features overlap. Define the scores: homogeneity asks whether each cluster holds one label (0.357), completeness whether each label sits in one cluster (0.386), V-measure is their harmonic mean (0.371), from 0 (random) to 1 (perfect). The answer to the opening question: two of the four groups come back without labels; the other two need labels, which is what supervised classification in 3.4 and 3.5 adds.

Same events, four ways of grouping

Method (k = 4) Homogeneity Completeness V-measure
k-means on 15 principal components 0.357 0.386 0.371
k-means on all 61 features 0.359 0.393 0.375
Ward (hierarchical) on 15 components 0.343 0.472 0.398
Gaussian mixture on 15 components 0.311 0.386 0.344

No method separates explosions from surface events; Ward scores highest only because one of its clusters holds most explosions and surface events together.

Two minutes. All four methods land between 0.34 and 0.40. Ward’s agglomerative clustering starts with every event alone and merges the pair of groups that increases the within-group variance least, until four remain. Its higher completeness comes from one very large cluster (929 explosions, 959 surface events and 527 earthquakes): keeping each label in one cluster is easy if one cluster holds almost everything. A Gaussian mixture lets each group have its own shape and size; here it does slightly worse. In the notebook, splitting the mixed cluster again into two does not separate explosions from surface events either. The scientific conclusion: with these 61 features, the difference between a blast and a rockfall is not where the variance is. A geoscientist’s next step would be features that target it, for example the frequency content in the first seconds, or the location and time of day used in Session 12.
