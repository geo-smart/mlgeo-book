Today’s question

A seismometer in Seattle records ocean waves, traffic, wind and earthquakes at the same time. Each of them is somebody’s measurement.

What is signal, what is noise, and who decides?

Lecture 8 dealt with problems made by the recording system: filter phase, gaps, clocks. Today the unwanted part of the record comes from the Earth or from people: the ocean microseism, traffic, wind, the atmosphere over a radar image, a spotted star, a quarry blast in an earthquake catalog. These are real signals of other sources that overlap the one we want in time, frequency, space or class. Calling them noise is a decision tied to a question. Arc of the hour: one real day of seismic data; published figures in 1D, 2D and categorical data where one study’s noise is another study’s signal; then synthetic versions of each, with the colors (spectra) and the value distributions that make them realistic; then teams build a synthetic version of their own project data. Data: UW.SP2 HHZ via EarthScope FDSN, doi:10.7914/SN/UW; USGS ComCat; NLNM and NHNM from Peterson (1993), USGS OFR 93-322; coastlines: Natural Earth. Figure: figscripts/lec09_day_spectrogram.py.

On one Thursday, daytime traffic raises the 1–10 Hz band by 9.8 dB while the ocean microseism stays within 1.5 dB of its night level, and two earthquakes cross both bands.

Three minutes. SP2 sits in Seward Park on the shore of Lake Washington (47.556°N, 122.249°W, station service coordinates), a Trillium Compact at 1 m depth. Each 5-minute window has a Welch power spectral density (PSD) of ground acceleration after removing the instrument response, in dB relative to 1 (m/s²)²/Hz. Panel (a): four contributions. Below 0.1 Hz the record is quiet except for the teleseism’s surface waves after 17:51. The band near 0.15 Hz (6.7 s) is the secondary ocean microseism: ocean waves meeting waves traveling the other way press on the seafloor, and the pressure couples into Rayleigh waves that cross the continent; it does not care about the time of day. The 1 to 30 Hz band rises through the morning, holds through the working day and drops at about 18:30 local time: traffic on the roads around the park, buses, industry, people. Two earthquakes: the ML 3.38 at 13:02:49 UTC (05:03 PST), 18.6 km deep and 41 km away, and the Mww 6.3 on the southern East Pacific Rise at 01:51 UTC on 23 February (17:51 PST), 83° away, whose surface waves show below 0.1 Hz. Panel (c): median day and night spectra against Peterson’s new low and high noise models (NLNM, NHNM), the envelope of quiet and noisy stations worldwide. Panel (b): band power through the day; the cultural band is 9.55 times louder by day (median 08 to 18 h against 00 to 05 h), and its 5-minute maximum is 5,225 times its minimum counting the ML 3.4 window, 131 without it (99th over 1st percentile: 88). The microseism ratio is 0.71. All numbers from the script’s run on 20 October 2026 (it fetches the data each time). Now the question: for an earthquake seismologist both bands are noise. For ambient-noise imaging the microseism is the signal (it is correlated between stations to map the crust); for an urban seismologist traffic is the signal; for a tsunami or slow-slip modeler the teleseism is a nuisance. Three minutes, two studies where the “noise” is the measurement. Left, Poli et al. (2020): the dips every seven days are weekends, the long dip is the Christmas break, and the slow decline from late February follows the decrees that closed northern Italy. Quotes: “We find that the lockdown reduces ambient noise significantly in the 1–10 Hz frequency range”; “The energy drop associated with the lockdown is comparable with that occurring every weekend and during the winter break”; natural sources “such as rain, wind and sea/ocean waves are typically characterized by frequencies below 1 Hz, and are obviously not affected by the lockdown.” In the text, anthropic noise dominates above about 2 Hz, “where most stations show a ~50% reduction” (that number comes from their Fig. 5, not this figure). The caption gives the bands as 1 to 5 and 10 to 15 Hz while the lower panel reads 5 to 15 Hz; quote the panel labels. The PSD axes carry no tick values. Right, Aster et al. (2023), panel (b) of their Fig. 7 (panels (a) and (c) are cropped out): 52 global seismographs, vertical primary microseism at 14 to 20 s, late 1980s to August 2022. The primary microseism is made where ocean waves shoal against the coast, so its amplitude records wave energy. In the cropped panel (a), seven station clusters track the ENSO (El Niño Southern Oscillation) index in the Southwest Pacific and the SOI (Southern Oscillation Index) in the Southeast Pacific and Southwest Atlantic; the El Niño and La Niña excursions A to E are marked on (b) too. Panel (b): the global median, trends 0.27 ± 0.03% per year for all data and 0.35 ± 0.04% per year since 2000. Increasing trends at 3σ at 41 stations (79%). Here the roles flip: earthquakes are the noise, and the authors “remove potential earthquake transients from global earthquakes with magnitudes ≥5.75” from the USGS catalog. Caveats: trends are normalized to 1 on 1 January 2005 and the blue and red lines are shifted by ±15% for clarity, so their offsets are not data. Planet, contamination and two spot crossings

Planet and moon

Kipping et al. (2022), Nature Astronomy 6, 367–380, Extended Data Fig. 3, cropped to two rows of the second transit, doi:10.1038/s41550-021-01539-1. CC BY 4.0.

Two starspot crossings fit the second transit better than an exomoon (χ² 702.9 against 712.1, 14 parameters each); the star varies by 10.9 mmag, the transit is 1.5 mmag deep.

Two and a half minutes. Lecture 8’s problems have no physical source outside the instrument: nobody wants the ringing of a filter. Rarely, not never: a clock error recovered from ambient-noise correlations is itself the measurement (Stehly et al. 2007; Sens-Schönfelder 2008). They are corrected or masked, and the processing history records what was done. A nuisance signal is somebody’s measurement, so you do not throw it away: you model it alongside your signal, or you name it as a class. The exoplanet case shows why this matters. Kepler measures starlight from one star; a planet crossing it dims the light by about 1.4 × 10⁻³ (~1.5 mmag). Kepler-150 f’s second transit has a floor that bounces up and down, which a moon trailing the planet could produce. But the star has “periodicity of 17.6 d and amplitude of 10.9 mmag. This far exceeds the depth of Kepler-150 f’s transit (~1.5 mmag)”. When a planet crosses a dark starspot, the dimming briefly weakens. The slide shows two of the six rows of their figure. All rows: planet only (AIC 746.3); plus contamination (738.9); plus 1, 2, 3 spots (736.3, 730.9, 734.0); planet plus moon (740.1). Quote: “a modification to the planet-only model that includes two spot crossings … yields a Δχ2 = 9.2 improved fit to the transits versus the planet–moon model, despite using the same number of free parameters”, and “we conclude that this is most probably spot-driven activity rather than an exomoon signature.” Starspots are the nuisance for a planet hunter and the signal for a stellar physicist, and the choice between “spot” and “moon” is a choice between classes made by comparing models. The paper’s headline candidate, Kepler-1708 b-i, is disputed in a later article; this Kepler-150 f result does not depend on that dispute. Exoplanet teams: the TESS template in 2.10 section 5.5 adds spots and granulation to a transit. Ask: in your data, which unwanted part has a source outside the instrument?

Figure: figscripts/lec09_noise_1d.py (seed 2026, 200 realizations per noise model). AR(1): first-order autoregressive, φ = 0.9.

At equal variance, the slow transient’s best-case SNR falls from 67.7 in white noise to 4.9 in flicker noise and 1.7 in a random walk, where the short pulse rises to 23.4.

Two and a half minutes. The color of noise is the shape of its power spectrum, P(f) ∝ f^slope. Panels and table rows are ordered by fitted slope. White noise has equal power at all frequencies: each day independent of the last. A first-order autoregressive process (AR(1), x[t] = 0.9 x[t−1] + e[t]), which climate papers call red noise, is white at long periods and falls off as about f⁻² above a corner near 0.017 cycles per day; AR(1) has a corner, so its fitted slope depends on the band (−0.12 below 0.01 cycles per day, −1.58 between 0.05 and 0.5, −1.49 over the whole band). Flicker noise (1/f, slope −1) has no corner: GNSS position series and many climate records look like this. A random walk (1/f², slope −2 in theory, −1.80 fitted over this band) is what you get by summing white noise: a drifting monument, a clock. Its unit variance holds only in expectation: across realizations its sample standard deviation spans 0.46 to 1.66 (5 to 95%), and it grows with record length. The two transients are the shapes geodesists look for: a short burst, like a swarm or a short slow-slip event, and a slow ramp over about two years, like postseismic relaxation. Both have the same peak, 2 noise standard deviations. SNR here is the matched-filter signal-to-noise ratio, sqrt(sᵀC⁻¹s): the best any detector can do if it knows the noise covariance exactly. Read the table by column. Slow transient: its power sits at low frequency, exactly where colored noise is strongest, so the SNR falls from 67.7 to 1.7. Pulse: a random walk is smooth from day to day, so a 4-day pulse stands out against it (23.4); AR(1) puts its power at periods longer than about 60 days, which overlaps the pulse’s spectrum (2.4). Sign conventions differ between fields: McPartland et al. (2025, Climate of the Past, doi:10.5194/cp-21-1917-2025) write S(f) ∼ f^−β with β = 1 “red” for tree rings and corals; GNSS papers write a spectral index κ = −1 for flicker noise; Masson et al. (2019, Solid Earth) use k negative. In physics and geodesy, β = 1 is pink or flicker, β = 2 red or Brownian. Ask: which transient would you miss in a GNSS series if you assumed white noise when estimating its uncertainty?

At the same 6 mm noise level, correlated noise lowers the correlation with the true bowl to 0.50, against 0.62 for white; decorrelation noise is 4.4 mm where coherence is below 0.5 and 0.8 mm on coherent ground.

Two minutes. The signal is the uplift above an inflating magma chamber, a Mogi point source (a pressure change in a small sphere) 4 km deep, scaled to 30 mm, the size of signals InSAR resolves at restless volcanoes; standard deviation over the grid 4.80 mm, half-peak radius 3.07 km. Three noises. White: independent pixels, 6 mm. Correlated: a power-law field with spectrum k^−8/3, also 6 mm; −8/3 is the slope Hanssen (2001) reports for tropospheric delay between about 0.5 and 6 km (3D turbulence), and at larger scales, which hold most of this 25.6 km grid’s variance, his slope is about −5/3. Its blobs have the size of the bowl, so it can make or hide a bowl (panel c); that is the InSAR atmosphere of the Rouet-Leduc slide. Decorrelation: when the ground surface changes between the two radar dates (vegetation, snow, farming), the interferometric phase becomes noisy. The noise is additive and set by the coherence γ, not by the deformation: phase standard deviation sqrt(1 − γ²)/(γ sqrt(2L)) (the Cramér-Rao bound, Rodriguez and Martin 1992; Hanssen 2001), with L = 4 looks, capped at the uniform-phase value π/√3, converted to line of sight with λ/4π for C band (λ = 55.5 mm, Sentinel-1). The coherence map is 0.9 on bare ground and falls to 0.2 in a vegetated patch 4 km east and 2 km north of the source: noise 0.79 mm where γ > 0.85, 4.42 mm where γ < 0.5 (6.6% of the grid), 7.65 mm at the patch centre. Real InSAR displacement has no multiplicative speckle: speckle multiplies radar intensity (slide 13), and decorrelation phase noise is additive and grows as coherence drops. The table’s noise spectrum slope is the fitted log-log slope of the radially averaged noise spectrum (panel f): 0 for white and decorrelation, −2.66 for the correlated field, while the signal falls steeply (−5.3 fitted, not a power law). The SNR labels in panels (b) to (d) are std(signal)/std(noise) and, in brackets, peak/std: 0.80 (5.0) for white and correlated, 3.16 (19.8) for decorrelation, whose noise is small on average and large where it matters. Same SNR, different damage: correlation with the true field 0.624 for white, 0.503 for correlated. Lesson: an SNR number does not describe 2D noise without its spatial spectrum. Rouet-Leduc et al. build their training synthetics the same way, with turbulent correlated noise, a topography-correlated delay and outlier pixels. Ask: which of the three would a smoothing filter remove, which would it keep, and which would a coherence mask remove?

Random flips of 30% cost the classifier 2.4 points of accuracy; class-dependent flips of 16% overall cost 11.8 points and lower explosion recall from 91% to 54%.

Two and a half minutes. Three source classes: earthquake, explosion, noise trigger, described by two features of the kind regional networks use, the log P/S amplitude ratio (explosions make relatively more P) and the log ratio of 5 to 10 Hz over 1 to 5 Hz energy (microseism-dominated noise windows are poorer in high frequencies). Both features are drawn from overlapping Gaussian clusters, so even the true labels allow 91.1% accuracy. Top row: what a user receives. Bottom row: confusion matrix of the label received (columns) against the true class (rows), in percent of each true class. Random flips change any label to any other class with equal probability: “noise completely at random” in the Frénay and Verleysen taxonomy. With many correct labels around them, wrong ones average out: 30% random noise costs 2.4 points. Class-dependent flips push one class into another, the way a quarry blast enters a catalog as an earthquake (the Chien and Liu slide; Wiemer and Baer 2000 on blast contamination of seismicity catalogs). The classifier learns the error: explosion recall 53.7%. Explosion recall rises slightly at 30% random flips (92.5% against 91.2%), within sampling noise for about 150 test explosions. Whether structured flips hurt accuracy depends on the classifier and the class geometry; in template E of notebook 2.10 a nearest-class-mean rule barely changes (0.75 to 0.76 at 15% wrong labels, mean of 20 catalogs) while random flips cost more (0.68), but the structured flips bias a science quantity, the earthquake day-to-night ratio (1.74 against 1.03). The slide uses log P/S and a frequency ratio; template E uses log S/P and the hour of day, a different feature set with the same lesson. The same pattern on real crop maps: Pelletier et al. (2017, Remote Sensing 9, 173, doi:10.3390/rs9020173, CC BY 4.0) injected label noise in land-cover training sets; “at 40% noise level, the RF OA values of the 5-class real dataset are equal to 72% and 60% for random and systematic noise respectively” (RF: random forest; OA: overall accuracy), and “the cross-validation procedure is impacted by the presence of class label noise”, because the validation labels are noisy too. In CloudSEN12 (Aybar et al. 2022, Scientific Data 9, 782), thin cloud is the class expert labelers changed most during their own quality control (producer’s accuracy 78% between labels before and after the check). Ask: in your catalog or map, which two classes would a tired analyst confuse?

The 2001 M6.8 Nisqually earthquake carries 98.8% of the moment of all 5,680 events; an exponential fit gives SeaTac’s 127.5 mm day a probability of 6.5 × 10⁻¹⁰, against 1 in 11,998 observed.

Three minutes. Color says how values are correlated; the distribution says how large single values get. They are independent properties. Top row: five synthetic distributions with mean (solid) and median (dotted). A Gaussian arises when many small independent contributions add up; its tails fall as e^(−x²/2), so 0.27% of values lie beyond 3 standard deviations. Laplace and Student t (ν = 3 degrees of freedom) are symmetric with heavier tails: transients, gusts, passing trucks. The kurtosis column gives the sample value and, in brackets, the population value: infinite for Student t with 3 degrees of freedom, about 111 for a log-normal with σ = 1, undefined for Pareto with α = 1.5 because its variance is infinite. So the sample numbers for those three are not estimates of anything finite; notebook 2.10 prints 18,335.5 for another Student t draw. Log-normal and Pareto histograms are on a log x axis. Log-normal arises from products of many factors (grain sizes, permeability, rain amounts): skewed, all positive, mean 1.67 against median 1.00. Pareto, a power law P(X > x) = x^−1.5, has a finite mean and no finite variance: its running standard deviation was 7.48 at n = 1,000, 5.98 at 5,000 and 26.04 at 20,000, and never settles. The fraction beyond 3 std is a poor measure for it because its std is itself inflated. Bottom left: on log-log axes of exceedance probability, a power law is a straight line. Bottom middle: earthquakes in the box from Portland to the Canadian border (45.5 to 49°N, 121 to 124°W, Mount St. Helens included), catalog magnitudes converted to moment with M0 = 10^(1.5M + 9.1) N m (Hanks and Kanamori 1979), from local and duration magnitudes, so this is implied moment. The Gutenberg-Richter b-value gives the slope −2b/3 = −0.82. Mc (magnitude of completeness) here is 2.1 from b-value stability; maximum curvature plus 0.2 gives 0.7 on this box because it holds the 2004 to 2008 Mount St. Helens swarm and mixes ml and md. b keeps rising with the cutoff (0.51 at 0.5, 1.71 at 3.0) and is flat only from 2.1 to 2.5 (1.225 to 1.243), so 1.23 is the value on a short plateau of a curved frequency-magnitude distribution, not a regional b-value; the 98.8% moment share does not depend on it. Bottom right: daily rain on wet days (≥ 0.3 mm) at SeaTac, 1948 to 2024: skewness 3.20, excess kurtosis 18.48; the observed curve bends away from the exponential at the high end. Why it matters for noise: a 3-sigma clip assumes a Gaussian. On the catalog it would discard the largest earthquakes, which are the physics; on rain, the floods. Missing small events bend a power law at the low end too: Bernard et al. (2021, Earth Surface Dynamics 9, 1013, doi:10.5194/esurf-9-1013-2021) show that a 2D landslide inventory from aerial images rolls over near 20 m² while a lidar 3D inventory follows the power law to its 20 m² limit; the rollover there comes from under-detection. Ask: in your data, would the largest value be an outlier to remove or the event you study?

Transform What happens to the noise Example
Average over L looks or dates variance divided by L if the looks or dates are independent and the scene unchanged (b): 45 dates of the 1-look image (a)

Dalsasso, Yang, Denis, Tupin & Yang (2020), Remote Sensing 12, 2636, Fig. 4, doi:10.3390/rs12162636. CC BY 4.0.

Averaging 45 Sentinel-1 dates divides speckle variance by 45 under Goodman’s model; taking the log makes speckle additive but leaves it non-Gaussian, with a biased mean.

Two minutes, optional: skip it if the clock is past 10:40. Every transform in the processing chain changes the noise you hand to a model. Panel (a): one Sentinel-1 radar image. Speckle is physical: “Because of the interference between echoes produced by elementary scatterers, the intensity fluctuates (speckle phenomenon).” Goodman’s model: y = x × n, with n gamma distributed, E[n] = 1 and Var[n] = 1/L, so “Var[y] = x²/L: the noise is signal-dependent”. Panel (b): “multitemporal averaging of 45 dates”, which works only “assuming that no changes occurred”; the variance falls by 45 only if the speckle is also independent between dates, which it is not on stable, coherent scatterers such as buildings. Panel (c): (b) denoised with MuLoG+BM3D, a filter that works on log-intensities. After the log, speckle is additive but follows a Fisher-Tippett distribution, and “The mean E[ne] = ψ(L) − log(L) (ψ is digamma function) is non-zero, which indicates that averaging log-transformed intensities ye leads to a biased estimate of the log-reflectivity” (their Fig. 3 compares it with a Gaussian for L = 1, 5 and 20). The authors note that calling speckle noise “is sometimes considered misleading in the context of SAR imaging”, which is today’s point again: for an analyst of surface roughness the speckle statistics are information. Other rows: differencing a random walk returns the white steps; differencing white noise multiplies its spectrum by 4 sin²(πf), so high frequencies grow (blue). A band-pass keeps only the noise in the band and makes it correlated over about one period (lecture 8’s ringing). A z-score changes no SNR, but a heavy-tailed record gets a large std from a few spikes. A spectrogram in dB is a log of power, so multiplicative changes become shifts; a single periodogram value is exponentially distributed, so its log is biased low by Euler’s constant, 0.577 in natural log or 2.5 dB. Ask: which transform in your processing would change the distribution of your noise the most?

Thomas, Melgar, Dybing & Searcy (2023), Seismica 2(1), Fig. 3, doi:10.26443/seismica.v2i1.240. CC BY 4.0.

Thomas et al. combined 822,256 synthetic three-component signals with 729,303 real noise records; at amplitude SNR 0.4 the denoised vertical correlates with the truth at −0.67.

Two minutes. The recipe is the one in notebook 2.10 section 5, and published studies follow it. Thomas et al. denoise high-rate GNSS (HR-GNSS) displacement, whose noise of “1 cm–2 cm” hides small earthquakes. Signals: simulated ruptures (“fakequakes”) of magnitude 4 to 7.2. Noise: real records from “the 278 GNSS stations closest to the Ridgecrest events” on days between 1 June 2019 and 31 July 2020 “that did not have an earthquake above M4.3”. Quote: “Training data are synthetic HR-GNSS records and actual noise recordings that are superimposed to generate noisy signals”; “The combinations of signal and noise and the time-shifting are data augmentation strategies.” The truth is known for every training sample, which is what makes the masks in columns 2 and 3 possible. Their SNR is an amplitude ratio: peak signal over the standard deviation of the pre-event noise. On this example, north and east (SNR 1.9 and 2.4) are recovered (CC, their correlation coefficient, 0.879 and 0.899); the vertical, SNR 0.4, gives CC = −0.674, a wrong waveform rather than a weak one. Their Fig. 2 shows another design choice: peak ground displacement is “nearly uniform … up to 1 m and then exponentially decreasing”, a training distribution chosen by the authors, unlike the power law of real earthquakes. Masson et al. (2019, Solid Earth 10, 329, doi:10.5194/se-10-329-2019) do the same for GNSS velocities: 3,600 synthetic daily series with a velocity, annual and semiannual terms, offsets and colored noise (Kasdin 1995 generator), and find that series shorter than 4.5 years “are not suitable for studies that require precisions lower than mm yr−1”. Step 6 is the course rule: a model validated only on synthetic data has not been validated. Ask: which of the six ingredients is hardest to make realistic for your project?

Left: figscripts/lec09_stalta_floor.py (notebook 2.10 section 4.4, same seeds). Right: Rouet-Leduc et al. (2021), Nature Communications 12, 6480, Fig. 2, doi:10.1038/s41467-021-26254-3. CC BY 4.0.

STA/LTA crosses 50% detection at amplitude SNR 4.97; the InSAR autoencoder recovers fault offsets down to a power SNR of about 0.5%, an RMS ratio near 0.07, using nine frames of 48 × 48 pixels.

Two minutes. Known truth lets you measure the weakest signal a method can see. Left, notebook 2.10 section 4.4: the noise is RATT’s with random phases (spectrum-matched, section 4.1). The threshold comes from noise alone: 200 noise-only windows, peak STA/LTA median 4.34, 99th percentile 6.09, so the false-alarm rate is 1% by design, before any event is planted. Then Ricker wavelets at 20 SNR levels, 60 trials each; SNR is peak amplitude over noise standard deviation. Detection is 0.07 at SNR 2.64, crosses 50% at 4.97 and reaches 1.00 at 16.24. These numbers were reproduced by rerunning the notebook offline on 20 October 2026 (CI=1, MLGEO_OFFLINE=1). Every catalog has such a floor; when it moves (new stations, more traffic), apparent seismicity rates change with no change in the Earth (Habermann 1987, Bulletin of the Seismological Society of America 77, 141–159). Right, Rouet-Leduc et al.: 10⁵ synthetic test series, each nine frames of 48 × 48 pixels with a fault offset, turbulent correlated noise, a topography-correlated delay and outlier pixels; “All other phase delays are identified as noise.” The y axis is the structural similarity index (SSIM) between output and truth. “For SNRs above 20%, our algorithm provides a very accurate reconstruction … (0.7 < SSIM < 1.0)”; “the overall deformation signal is recovered down to SNRs of ~0.5%”. Their SNR is a power ratio, RMS²(signal)/RMS²(noise), so 20% is an RMS ratio of about 0.45 and 0.5% about 0.07; STA/LTA’s SNR on the left is peak amplitude over noise standard deviation, a different definition. Examples 2 and 3 (SNR about 2 × 10⁻², 4 × 10⁻³, read from the plot) show no visible offset in the inputs. The two floors are not a ranking: the autoencoder pools 9 × 48 × 48 samples and was trained on noise drawn from the same generator as its test set, and the paper states the assumption that atmospheric noise is “second-order stationary”. Ask: what would happen to the right-hand floor if the real atmosphere were not stationary?
